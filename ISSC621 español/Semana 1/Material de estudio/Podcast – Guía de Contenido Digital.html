Semana 1
Introducción a la Informática Forense Digital

 

Hey—welcome to Computer Forensics, and welcome to Week 1: An Introduction to Digital Forensics.

Alright, let’s start with the simplest truth about digital forensics: it’s not about being clever. It’s about being disciplined.

People sometimes imagine digital forensics as this dramatic thing—someone in a hoodie, typing fast, pulling secrets out of a computer like it’s a magic trick. But if you try to do real digital forensics that way, you’ll get two outcomes:

  • you’ll contaminate evidence
  • and nobody will trust your conclusions

So, your job in Week 1 is to build the mindset that protects truth.

Because digital forensics is really the process of finding truth in systems that change constantly.

A computer changes when it runs.

A phone changes when it receives notifications.

A cloud system changes when someone logs in;, a token refreshes, or a file syncs. Even opening a file can change timestamps or create new artifacts.

So, if you’re careless, you can accidentally create the story you think you’re investigating.

That’s why digital forensics is a process.

What digital forensics actually is

Digital forensics is a disciplined approach to:

  • identify potential evidence
  • Preserve it
  • collect it
  • examine and analyze it
  • and report what you found in a way that is credible and defensible

Notice what’s missing from that definition: “run a tool.”

Tools matter. But tools are not the job.

The job is:

  • evidence integrity
  • repeatability
  • and documentation

In other words: if someone else looked at the same evidence using your method, would they find the same thing?

That’s repeatability.

And could you explain your steps clearly enough that a skeptical person would believe you didn’t “make it up”?

That’s defensibility.

Why “preserve first” changes everything

Let’s talk about the preserve-first mindset, because it’s the number one habit that separates a forensics professional from a curious person.

Imagine you arrive at a scene—maybe it’s a workplace, maybe it’s an internal investigation, maybe it’s a legal case. Someone points at a laptop and says: “That’s the one. That’s the device. Just open it and look.”

That moment is the test.

If you treat the laptop like a normal computer, you’ll start clicking around. You’ll open the browser. You’ll look at downloads. You’ll search the file system.

And you’ll change the system as you do it.

Even if you don’t mean to.

So, preserve-first means: before you analyze, you protect integrity.

That includes:

  • documenting what you see and the condition of the device
  • recording the time and who is present
  • preventing further changes when possible
  • establishing chain of custody
  • planning for controlled acquisition

This isn’t about being slow. It’s about being reliable.

Because speed without integrity is just guessing faster.

Chain of custody: not “paperwork,” but credibility

Let’s be real—people don’t love the chain of custody. It feels like paperwork. It feels like bureaucracy.

But the chain of custody exists because evidence gets challenged.

Sometimes evidence gets challenged in court.

Sometimes it gets challenged internally. Sometimes HR asks questions. Sometimes leadership asks questions. Sometimes another investigator has to take over.

Chain of custody answers the question: Who had the evidence, when, where, and why?

And without that record, your work becomes vulnerable to doubt:

  • “How do we know this wasn’t altered?”
  • “How do we know you didn’t miss something?”
  • “How do we know someone else didn’t handle it before you?”
  • “How do we know the device you analyzed is the same one from the scene?”

The chain of custody protects you, too.

It’s not about proving you’re perfect. It’s about proving you were careful and accountable.

Evidence integrity: the promise you make to the truth

When you work in digital forensics, you are making a promise: “I did not change the evidence in a way that changes the story.”

That’s integrity.

And because digital systems change naturally, integrity doesn’t mean “nothing ever changed.” It means: changes are controlled, prevented when possible, and documented when they occur.

That’s why you’ll often hear about using controlled acquisition methods and validation techniques—like verifying that what you collected matches what you intended to collect.

The deeper you go into the course, the more you’ll learn about how investigators protect integrity technically. But in Week 1, your focus is the mindset: protect first, analyze second.

What counts as evidence? More than you think

One more thing before we wrap Week 1: evidence is not only files.

Digital evidence includes:

  • logins and authentication events
  • system logs
  • file metadata (timestamps, ownership, access patterns)
  • browser artifacts
  • cloud sync activity
  • application logs
  • connected device history
  • network activity indicators
  • and evidence of deletion or concealment

That matters because beginners often focus only on “the file.” Like, “Where is the stolen file?”

But a lot of cases are solved by patterns:

  • repeated access at odd hours
  • logins from unusual locations
  • data transfers that don’t match normal behavior
  • deleted artifacts that still leave traces
  • or configuration decisions that allowed access without permission

So, Week 1 is about building the foundation that lets you interpret those patterns later.

A realistic mental rehearsal

Let’s do a quick mental rehearsal.

Someone says: “We think this employee is stealing data.”

Your job is not to accuse. Your job is to preserve and find evidence.

So, you ask:

  • What device or account is involved?
  • What systems did the person have access to?
  • What is the time window?
  • What evidence sources exist—device, cloud logs, access records?
  • What is at risk right now—could evidence be deleted or overwritten?
  • What is the preserve-first plan?

That’s the mental model you’re building.

Closing

So, as you finish Week 1, here’s the takeaway I want you to keep close:

Digital forensics is the discipline of protecting truth.

If you preserve evidence, maintain chain of custody, document your actions, and aim for repeatability, you give your findings credibility. And credibility is what makes your work matter—whether the investigation stays internal or ends up in a formal legal process.

Next week, you’ll move into Week 2: Types of Internet Crimes & Evidence, where you’ll start connecting crime types to the digital traces they leave behind—and you’ll practice thinking like an investigator who knows where to look, not just how to look.

You’re off to a strong start. Keep your mindset disciplined, and everything you learn next will land with more power.
