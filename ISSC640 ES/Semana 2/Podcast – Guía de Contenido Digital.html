Fundamentos de Comunicación de Datos: LAN, MAN y WAN

 

Welcome back to ISSC640. This is Week 2, and the topic is Data Communication Basics: LAN, MAN, and WAN. In Week 1, you looked at the history of the internet and the trends shaping modern networks. This week you shift into a skill you will use constantly in networking: understanding network scale and tracing the path that data follows from a user to a destination. 

When people experience a network problem, they often describe it with one word: “slow.” Your job, as someone learning networking fundamentals, is to turn “slow” into a more precise diagnosis. Is the problem inside the building, across a provider link, or at the destination service. Does the problem involve bandwidth, latency, jitter, or packet loss? When you can answer those questions, you can troubleshoot efficiently and communicate clearly.  

Among your Week 3 goals, you fill focus on three concepts. First, you will learn the difference between LAN, MAN, and WAN networks and what those categories mean in practice. Second, you will learn basic data communication characteristics, such as bandwidth and latency, and why they matter. Third, you will practice mapping communication paths and choosing the right metrics based on where the traffic travels.  

By the end of the week, you should be able to look at a scenario and say: this likely lives in the LAN, or it likely involves the WAN, and here is how I would confirm it.  

Let us start with the three terms: LAN, MAN, and WAN.  

A LAN, or local area network, is the network you control inside a small geographic area, like a home, an office floor, a building, or a campus segment. A LAN typically uses switches and wireless access points to connect devices. The LAN is usually where you have the most control, because you own the equipment and you can see the configuration.  

A MAN, or metropolitan area network, connects sites across a city or a regional area. Think of multiple buildings in the same city that need to share connectivity. Not every organization uses the term MAN daily, but the concept matters. A connection across a city often has different characteristics than an internal LAN. It may use provider infrastructure, shared fiber, or regional services.  

A WAN, or wide area network, connects sites across long distances. A WAN often relies heavily on service providers. If you connect a branch office in one state to a headquarters in another, or if you connect an office to a major cloud region, you are relying on WAN characteristics. A WAN often has higher delay than a LAN, and troubleshooting requires you to consider external dependencies. 

Now, in real environments, these categories overlap. An organization might have a LAN in each site, a provider network connecting sites regionally, and WAN connectivity to cloud services and remote offices. That is why the key skill is not memorizing a definition. The key skill is identifying where the traffic goes and which segments it crosses.  

Once you can name the segment, you can think about data communication characteristics.  

The first characteristic is bandwidth. Bandwidth is how much data can be transmitted per unit of time. People often think of bandwidth as the primary measure of network quality. It matters, but it is not the whole story. Bandwidth is especially important when many users share a link or when applications move large amounts of data. If a WAN circuit is small and many people are using video meetings, file sync, and cloud tools at the same time, bandwidth can become saturated. When bandwidth saturates, queues form, delays increase, and packet loss can appear.  

The second characteristic is latency. Latency is the delay between sending and receiving. Latency is influenced by distance, routing, and processing. On a LAN, latency is usually low. Across a WAN, latency is higher because traffic travels farther and crosses more devices. Latency matters for interactive applications. A file download can tolerate higher latency, but interactive work, like voice, video, and remote desktops, can feel uncomfortable when latency grows.  

The third characteristic is jitter, which is variation in latency. Jitter is especially important for real-time applications. Even if average latency is acceptable, high jitter can cause choppy audio and unstable video. This is why a speed test can show high bandwidth, but a voice call still sounds bad. The timing variation is the problem.  

The fourth characteristic is packet loss. Packet loss means some data never arrives. Loss can occur because of congestion, poor wireless conditions, interface errors, or other issues. Many modern applications can recover from small amounts of loss, but real-time services degrade quickly. Loss also forces retransmission, which increases delay and reduces effective throughput.  

Now, let us connect these characteristics back to LAN, MAN, and WAN troubleshooting.  

When you troubleshoot a LAN issue, you often start with local signals: switch status, Wi-Fi health, local utilization, and device connectivity. You ask: are there errors on interfaces, is a wireless channel crowded, are users far from access points, is there interference, is a loop causing broadcast storms, or is a device misconfigured. 

When you troubleshoot a WAN issue, you look at provider link metrics: bandwidth usage, latency, error rates, and packet loss across the circuit. You also look for time patterns. WAN problems often show up during peak hours when links are saturated. That is why comparing performance at different times can reveal congestion. WAN troubleshooting also often requires escalation. A provider might need to investigate routing or link health. The best escalations include evidence: timestamps, measured loss, and documented symptoms.  

For MAN-like connections, you blend both. You might have more provider control than a long-distance WAN, but you still rely on external infrastructure. The mindset is the same: identify the segment and measure what matters.  

Now, let us practice the most valuable habit for Week 2: mapping the communication path.  

Pick an everyday activity: a user joins a video meeting. What is the path?. It begins on the user’s device. It moves across the local Wi-Fi or wired LAN. It passes through a router or firewall at the site. It crosses the internet provider network, which is part of the WAN. It reaches the video service infrastructure, which could be cloud-hosted. That is the end-to-end path.  

Now, if the user says the meeting is choppy, you can test each segment. Is Wi-Fi stable? Is the site router overloaded? Is the WAN link saturated? Is there measured packet loss? Is latency unusually high? Are other sites affected? If only one site is affected, that points to local or WAN issues. If many sites are affected, that points to a shared external dependency or the service itself.  

This is why comparing sites is powerful. If the headquarters is fine and the branch office is struggling, you can focus on what differs. Often, the difference is bandwidth plan, Wi-Fi environment, or local network design. This reduces guesswork.  

Let us talk about common failure patterns and how they relate to LAN and WAN.  

A classic pattern is peak-hour slowdown at a branch office. During the day, performance is fine. During midday, when video meetings and file syncing increase, performance collapses. That often indicates WAN saturation. The fix might be more bandwidth, traffic prioritization, or scheduling large transfers outside peak periods.  

Another pattern is wireless instability. A user moves around, and connectivity drops. Or many users connect, and performance becomes inconsistent. That often indicates Wi-Fi congestion, poor placement, interference, or configuration issues. The fix might involve access point placement, channel planning, or upgrading capacity.  

Another pattern is high latency to distant services. If a user is far from a cloud region, latency can be higher. Applications that require frequent interaction can feel slower, even if bandwidth is adequate. The fix might involve choosing closer service regions, optimizing routing, or using performance acceleration techniques, depending on the environment.  

Now, a final theme for Week 2 is communication. Networking is technical, but support work is also communication. When you can explain, “This issue appears on the WAN link because we see bandwidth saturation and increased packet loss during peak hours,” you build trust. When you can document the path and show metrics, you can escalate effectively. When you can explain the difference between bandwidth and latency, users better understand why a high-speed connection can still feel slow.  

In Week 3, you will build on Week 2 by studying protocols, ports, and the OSI model. Those tools give you a structured way to locate failures at different layers, from physical links to application services. Week 2 gives you the geography, LAN, MAN, and WAN. Week 3 gives you the layered model to diagnose what is failing within those segments.  

Let us close with reflection questions.  

Choose a common task, such as loading a cloud file or joining a video meeting. Describe the path in three segments: local network, provider network, and destination service.  

Now name two performance factors most likely to affect the task. For video, think latency, jitter, and loss. For file transfers, think bandwidth and congestion. For login, think latency and reliability.  

Finally, if the task fails at one site but not another, what is the first difference you would investigate.  

If you can answer those questions, you are building the Week 2 habit: trace the path, measure what matters, and let evidence guide your troubleshooting.
