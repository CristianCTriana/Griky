Intrusion Detection: A Primer on Attackers and Their Methods

Intrusion Detection is the art and science of identifying unauthorized attempts to access, manipulate, or disable computer systems. To defend effectively, one must understand the adversary’s mindset and the technical fingerprints they leave behind during an intrusion.

The Adversary: Who and Why?

Modern intrusions are rarely random. They are driven by specific threat actors with varying levels of sophistication and intent:

  • Nation-State Actors (APTs): Highly sophisticated, well-funded, and patient. Their goal is usually long-term espionage or intellectual property theft.
  • Cybercriminals: Primarily motivated by profit. They favor high-impact attacks like ransomware or credential theft for sale on the dark web.
  • Hacktivists: Motivated by political or social causes. They often focus on disruptive attacks, such as Website defacement or Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS).
  • Insiders: Employees or contractors with legitimate access who abuse their privileges for personal gain or revenge.

Anatomy of an Intrusion

Attackers generally follow a structured lifecycle, often mapped to the Cyber Kill Chain or the MITRE ATT&CK framework. Detection is most effective when it catches the attacker early in this cycle.

  1. Reconnaissance: Passive or active gathering of information (scanning for open ports, researching employees).
  2. Weaponization & Delivery: Sending a malicious payload (phishing email, infected USB).
  3. Exploitation: Triggering the vulnerability (buffer overflows, SQL injection).
  4. Installation & Persistence: Ensuring they stay in the system even after a reboot (creating new user accounts, modifying startup tasks).
  5. Command & Control (C2): The infected system "calls home" to the attacker's server for instructions.
  6. Actions on Objectives: The final goal (exfiltrating data, encrypting files, or lateral movement).

Detection Methodologies

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) use two primary methods to catch these stages in action:

Comparison of Detection Methods
Methodology How it Works Pros Cons
Signature-Based Matches activity against a database of known "bad" patterns (like an antivirus). Very fast; low false-positive rate for known threats. Cannot detect "Zero-Day" (new) attacks.
Anomaly-Based Learns what "normal" looks like and flags anything that deviates. Can catch brand-new, never-before-seen attack on TTPs. High false-positive rate; requires a "training" period.

Key Indicators (The "Smoking Gun")

When an intrusion occurs, attackers leave behind Indicators of Compromise (IOCs). Effective detection focuses on:

  • Network Artifacts: Unusual outbound traffic to unknown countries, spikes in data transfer, or DNS queries for non-existent domains.
  • Host Artifacts: Unexpected registry changes, new large files in temporary directories, or processes running from unusual locations (e.g., cmd.exe running from a web server).
  • Log Inconsistencies: Successful logins at 3:00 AM from a location the user has never visited, or multiple failed login attempts followed by a success (Brute Force).
