Week 5 Common Attack Patterns

Profiling Attack Patterns

Take note that not all attack patterns are technology-based. In fact, regardless of your organization’s size, insider threats and attacks can have a pattern of their own. Although we need to use our intrusion detection methods to observe patterns of attack, without performing control testing, an insider could be a vulnerability that exploits our resources (Edwards, 2025).

Profiling attack patterns is the process of documenting and analyzing the repeatable methods adversaries use to exploit vulnerabilities.

By shifting focus from who the attacker is to how they behave, security teams can move from reactive blocking to proactive, behavior-based defense.

  • 1. The Core Frameworks

    To profile effectively, the industry relies on standardized "dictionaries" of adversary behavior:

    • MITRE ATT&CK®: A globally accessible knowledge base of adversary Tactics (the "why" or goal) and Techniques (the "how"). It maps the entire post-compromise lifecycle, from Initial Access to Exfiltration.
    • CAPEC™ (Common Attack Pattern Enumeration and Classification): A dictionary specifically focused on Attack Patterns. While ATT&CK focuses on broad behaviors, CAPEC provides a "blueprint" for specific exploits (e.g., SQL Injection, Cross-Site Scripting).
    • The Diamond Model: A framework used to link four core features of an intrusion: Adversary, Capability, Infrastructure, and Victim. This helps analysts see how an attack pattern shifts over time.

  • 3. The Profiling Workflow

    Effective profiling follows a repeatable analytical loop:

    1. Data Collection: Gathering raw logs (SIEM, EDR), network traffic, and external threat feeds.
    2. Pattern Recognition: Identifying clusters of activity. For example, if an attacker always registers a domain 48 hours before a campaign, that is a recognizable "tempo" or pattern.
    3. TTP Mapping: Mapping observed behaviors to the MITRE ATT&CK matrix.
    4. Hypothesis Generation: Using the Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) to determine the most likely intent based on the observed patterns.
    5. Defensive Tuning: Creating behavioral alerts (e.g., Sigma or YARA rules) that trigger on the pattern rather than just a specific IP address.

  • 4. Why Behavioral Profiling Wins

    Profiling attack patterns is superior to traditional "Indicator-based" defense because of the Pyramid of Pain.

    • Low Level: Blocking Hash values and IP addresses is "easy" for the defender, but "trivial" for the attacker to change.
    • High Level: Detecting Tactic, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs) is "tough" for the defender to build, but it is "painful" for the attacker to change, as it forces them to learn an entirely new way of operating.
