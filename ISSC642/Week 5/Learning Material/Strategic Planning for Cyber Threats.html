Week 5 Common Attack Patterns

Planning for Cyber Threats

Without testing, the best controls can be vulnerable to data breaches and financial loss (Edwards, 2025). Oftentimes, we will experience leadership in our organizations that does not want to test these controls due to the loss of productivity. However, it is our job as cybersecurity professionals to advise that the loss will be far greater if our intrusion methods are not tested for a certain attack that could cause a massive breach in the organization.

Effective cyber threat planning shifts an organization from a reactive "break-fix" mentality to a proactive "resilience" posture.

This requires a synchronized effort between technical leadership and executive stakeholders to ensure that security investments align with business risk.

  • 1. The Threat Modeling Process

    Planning begins with Threat Modeling, where an organization identifies its high-value assets (the "crown jewels") and maps out likely attack paths. By using frameworks, planners can visualize how an adversary might move through their specific environment.

  • 2. Defensive Strategy: Layered Resilience

    A robust plan assumes that a breach is inevitable. This "Assume Breach" mentality leads to a Defense-in-Depth architecture, where multiple layers of controls—Physical, Technical, and Administrative—work in tandem.

    • Preventative: Firewalls, MFA, and automated patching
    • Detective: SIEM, EDR, and continuous monitoring
    • Corrective: Incident Response plans and offline backups

  • 3. Continuous Validation

    A plan is only as good as its last test. Organizations must move beyond static policies and engage in Continuous Security Validation:

    • Tabletop Exercises (TTX): Non-technical simulations where leadership discusses how they would handle a crisis (e.g., a ransomware attack).
    • Red Teaming: Active, offensive simulations designed to test the effectiveness of the people, processes, and technology in place.

  • 4. Metrics and Maturity

    Finally, planners must track progress using maturity models like the CMMC or the NIST Cybersecurity Framework. By measuring "Mean Time to Detect" (MTTD) and "Mean Time to Respond" (MTTR), leadership can quantify the effectiveness of their threat planning over time.
