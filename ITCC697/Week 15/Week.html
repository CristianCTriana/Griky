  1. Tell them what you’re going to tell them
  2. Tell them
  3. Tell them what you told them

| Week 15 Relevance: The Rule of Three

The Rule of Three connects classical rhetoric, modern communication practice, and the structure of this course into a single, coherent framework for effective persuasion. As described through Aristotle’s model of introduction, proof, and conclusion, the rule provides a time-tested structure for organizing ideas so that audiences can easily follow, understand, and remember them. Marshall’s application of this rule aligns directly with how you progress through ITCC697: the proposal introduces the problem, the business case develops and justifies the solution, and the pitch deck summarizes the most important points for decision-makers. Across mathematics, law, and professional communication, the rule of three consistently serves to impose clarity and emphasis, reinforcing its reliability as more than a stylistic choice—it is a strategic method for making complex ideas accessible.

These ideas matter because approval of an IT solution depends as much on how it is communicated as on the solution's technical merit. Stakeholders rarely engage with lengthy documents in full; instead, they rely on clear summaries, visual cues, and concise narratives to make decisions. The pitch deck, when built using the rule of three, forces you to distill extensive research and analysis into focused, persuasive messages that highlight the problem, the solution, and the value proposition. By learning to communicate in this structured way, you will develop a professional skill that directly supports your ability to gain buy-in, justify investments, and lead projects forward. Ultimately, the rule of three helps you move from simply doing analysis to influencing outcomes, which is essential for success in both academic and real-world IT environments.
