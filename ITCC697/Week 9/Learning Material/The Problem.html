1. Perceiving the Problem

Many times, a problem or opportunity is seen from one perspective or within the narrow confines of one part of an overall process.

Knowing that your view might be constrained, you should strive to change your perspective and look at the problem in another way.

2. Processing the Problem

This is about how you process the problem, not a problem with the process.

Why does this particular problem intrigue you? It is important to understand how and why this came to your attention.

Are there similar problems out there? Part of understanding the problem is understanding where else it occurs or who else encounters it.

From your perspective, how big or involved is this problem? Is it like building a doghouse, or a stadium? Playing a game of “what if,” if this problem goes away, what do you think might happen? This provides you with a sense of scope and scale of the change.

3. Knowing the Problem

There is a danger inherent in “knowing the problem.” Many times, projects are initiated because someone of influence did a similar thing for another organization.

A problem may be like another one you’ve encountered, but if that was from another time and at another place, then it isn’t, in fact, the same problem. Where you are now, the people who are involved, and the work being done are all different to the prior case.
