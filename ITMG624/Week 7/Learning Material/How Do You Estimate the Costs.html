How Do You Estimate the Costs

Estimating Duration and Cost

How do you determine the activity duration (which builds the schedule), and how do you estimate the costs associated with any one activity that gets rolled into the project budget?

This week, you will explore these questions while talking about both the schedule and the budget.

Hi, welcome to a presentation where we talk about one of the biggest challenges that we face as project managers.

How do we determine activity durations, which will, of course, build the schedule?

Then, equally challenging, how do we estimate the cost associated with any activity so that when you roll all the costs up, it creates the project budget?

Why is it so important that we, as project managers, document very accurately and very realistically the activity duration as well as the cost for each activity?

It might sound silly to pose the question, but why is it important?

If you read the literature about why projects fail, the lion’s share of failure is tied to either poor scheduling techniques or poor budgeting techniques.

If they are not accurate, your project is doomed before it even starts.

Here we are reminded that accurate estimating supports good decision‑making.

When an organization is committed to exceptional estimating, it provides the foundation for successful projects.

It allows us to go into the project and schedule the work.

With each activity, we determine how long the project should take and how much it will cost.

From an organizational perspective, it helps determine whether the project is worth doing.

When thinking about how the organization must fund the project, estimating helps develop cash‑flow needs.

Sound estimating for activity duration and cost becomes another foundation for earned value analysis.

So what are some factors that influence quality estimates?

Panning is an overestimation—for example, you think an activity will take four days but you put down six days to be safe.

Panning might create built‑in risk management because you are overestimating the time required.

You might also think the cost will be five dollars but you put down eight dollars just to be safe.

Building a schedule and a budget under overly safe parameters might initially sound good.

But when your organization is competing for a contract—for example, a government contract to create a cybersecurity software system—the proposal will be evaluated based on cost and duration.

If you pad your schedule and budget to create a comfort buffer, you may not be competitive.

Next is culture.

An organization’s culture might be the opposite of padding; it might understate both schedule and cost to appear to be the low‑cost leader.

It might propose a schedule that is impossible to execute, appearing to deliver the fastest and cheapest project.

The intention would be to win the contract and then request overages later.

Other non‑project factors include inflation, which can cause unexpected cost spikes.

The cost of materials may increase more than expected.

The cost of fuel may impact projects requiring heavy equipment.

Logistics issues, such as supply‑chain delays, may affect estimates.

Government regulations might change and cause schedule delays.

The planning horizon refers to how much time and resource the organization invests in sound planning.

Complex projects with many moving parts can impact the quality of estimates.

People factors include availability of specialized skill sets, such as inspectors or crane operators.

Project structure within the organization also influences estimating.

If all decisions must go up to executives, decision delays may impact the project.

If people are not assigned full‑time to the project, progress may be slowed.

What are some guidelines for estimating?

Ultimately we are looking at activity duration, activity cost, and resource needs.

The project manager might understand the project but rely on experts who know how to do the work.

Schedules should always be based on normal conditions.

Padding or creating unrealistic schedules should be avoided.

Normal conditions may include recognizing weather impacts.

Use consistent units of measurement.

Treat each task independently and examine interdependencies.

Do not make early allowances for contingencies because those will be developed through risk management.

Always conduct a risk assessment and create a risk register.

The work package is the lowest level of the work breakdown structure.

Best practices include assuming tasks are independent, identifying resources and costs, and making no allowance for contingencies.

Use experts familiar with each task.

Use normal rather than optimal conditions.

Use consistent time units across the project.

Where do we start with developing a budget?

There are two general approaches: top‑down and bottom‑up.

A top‑down estimate means the organization gives a total amount—such as $125,000—and the project manager must determine whether the work can be done within that amount.

A bottom‑up estimate prices out all activities at the work‑package level and rolls them up through the WBS.

Top‑down is good for strategic decisions, high uncertainty, small internal projects, or unstable scope.

Bottom‑up is best when both budget and schedule accuracy are extremely important.

Bottom‑up is used for fixed‑price contracts.

The customer may want detailed schedules and budgets.

Preferred estimating approaches may begin with rough top‑down estimates and then refine with bottom‑up calculations.

You would create your WBS down to the work‑package level, gather schedule and cost details, then roll everything up.

You would reconcile differences between top‑down and bottom‑up numbers.

Top‑down estimates are conceptual and not very detailed.

You might use consensus methods, ratio methods, function‑point analysis, or learning‑curve principles.

The learning curve suggests performance improves as tasks are repeated.

Bottom‑up is used for detailed budgets and schedules and for determining resource requirements.

Preparation costs are higher because the estimate is more accurate.

Possible methods include using templates, parametric estimating, and previous project data adjusted for the current project.

Ranges of estimates can be used for activities to account for uncertainty.

Costs beyond direct project costs include direct labor, materials, and equipment.

There are direct project overhead costs, such as the project manager’s salary.

There are general and administrative overhead costs like rent, utilities, HR, finance, and executive salaries.

These overhead costs must be accounted for when creating proposals.

That covers some of the impacts and factors involved in producing reliable and accurate estimates for activities, schedules, and budgets.
