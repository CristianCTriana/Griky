Categorical Models vs. Dimensional Models

The debate between categorical versus dimensional models of psychopathology is significant and complex, with far-reaching theoretical, empirical, and clinical ramifications. Historically, most editions of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) have followed a categorical approach to diagnosis and classification, as exemplified by the disorder criteria outlined in the DSM-5 (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). Under this model, mental disorders are presumed to be qualitatively distinct from normal psychological processes and experiences. The primary criterion for diagnosis is whether a client meets the necessary diagnostic threshold; in other words, clients endorse or do not endorse enough criteria for a disorder to merit a formal diagnosis (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). In this regard, disorders are discrete and present all or nothing.

As a diagnostic system, categorical models offer benefits for administration, standardization, and relative ease of use. However, over the years, numerous conceptual, empirical, and clinical critiques of this approach have emerged and been debated, with no end in sight. For example, in a categorical system, one of the most saliant areas of concern is comorbidity or the high co-occurrence of different disorders has been one of the most salient areas of concern (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

Closely related to this issue is a lack of within-diagnosis homogeneity, which has been increasingly well-documented (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). That is, within a single disorder, individuals with the same diagnosis may present with disparate symptoms, levels of impairment, and clinical needs.

If, in reality, mental disorders are distinct in their etiology, pathophysiological processes, and natural course of illness, the rates of diagnostic overlap observed in categorical systems are perplexing and theoretically problematic. In practice, co-occurrence is common (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

Dimensional models offer a contrast to categorical diagnosis and classification, as they focus on the continuum of psychopathological characteristics that range from normal variation to pathological impairment. Rather than adhering to fixed cutoffs for formal diagnoses, dimensional models give weight to considerations of symptom severity, trait expression, and functional impairment. Conceptually, dimensional models of classification have the potential to retain more clinically relevant information, minimize arbitrary cutoffs, and better approximate the underlying biological, psychological, and social factors that are especially apparent for complex and personality psychopathology (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

The divergence in classification systems targets the notion of discreteness, which directly informs diagnostic decision-making and, by extension, theoretical considerations of continuous psychological constructs and traits. For this reason, gaining an understanding of categorical versus dimensional approaches to classification is expected to align with graduate-level training learning objectives on the history and classification of psychopathology.

Diagnostic Conceptualizations

To provide some additional explanation of the differences between categorical and dimensional models, please consider the example noted below.

Case Example

A 32-year-old reports the following symptoms present in the past two months: depressed mood on most days, fatigue, insomnia, and diminished ability to concentrate. The client endorses moderate levels of distress and functional impairment at work. Anhedonia, suicidal ideation, psychomotor agitation/retardation, and changes in appetite are not endorsed.

Categorical Approach

In a categorical system the client does not meet full criteria for a diagnosis, in this case major depressive disorder. As a result, a major depressive disorder diagnosis cannot be rendered regardless of distress or impairment. Other specified depressive disorders may be diagnosed, or a formal diagnosis may be omitted.

Dimensional Approach

The client is conceptualized in a dimensional framework as having moderate depressive symptom severity on a continuum of mood symptoms. Symptom severity, duration, and functional impairment are measured with scales such as the PHQ 9 as opposed to being qualitatively determined against a diagnostic threshold. Treatment planning is based on symptom severity and functional impairment, regardless of whether diagnostic criteria are met.
