Classification: Why Is It Important?

In the psychiatric context, classification can be defined as the process of specifying constructs and drawing boundaries between different diagnostic concepts. The process of classification makes the claim of what is and is not a mental disorder, which, in turn, defines what the field of psychopathology is, what its scope is, and what it assumes about psychological processes (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). As the process of conceptualizing and classifying disorders necessarily drives how those disorders are conceptualized, investigated, and treated, psychiatric classification has had a formative influence on both clinical practice and empirical research.

Classification provides a standard professional language.

Diagnostic systems offer a shared terminology that clinicians and researchers can use to communicate about complex clinical phenomena in a concise and consistent manner. A shared diagnostic system provides the framework for communication across different professional disciplines and treatment settings, but also with clients and families, with insurance and policy institutions (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). In the absence of such a system, clinical communication would be confined to highly idiosyncratic and personalized case descriptions.

Diagnostic systems also have an important organizational role. Diagnostic constructs act as anchor points that allow for efficient retrieval of information about etiology, treatment, and prognosis (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). In this way, classification systems structure clinical decision-making, research, and training. In a digital information age, diagnostic constructs have also taken on a more practical role as navigation tools, guiding access to information in scholarly literature and, increasingly, in public discourse (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

Systematic description is another primary function of psychiatric classification.

Description is the necessary foundation of the science of psychopathology; scientific understanding of any phenomenon necessarily builds on the ability to organize observations of that phenomenon in consistent, replicable ways. Diagnostic categories are meant to group people who are similar along clinically meaningful dimensions, while separating them from people who differ along those same dimensions (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). By enabling the comparison of cases, the organization of information about cases into categories and subcategories—and the identification of patterns—classification makes possible the search for regularities and generalizability.

Prediction is a particularly important function of classification in a clinical context.

Diagnoses are expected to help inform treatment selection, case management, and anticipation of illness course and outcome. A classification system is most useful for these clinical purposes to the extent that its diagnostic distinctions map onto differential response to treatment or different developmental trajectories (with or without treatment) (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

Classification systems can also play a role in theory development by organizing clinical phenomena in a manner that provokes questions about the processes that underlie them. In many scientific disciplines, classification has been a necessary prerequisite for later theory development. Psychopathology is no exception, and the way in which it is classified and organized has necessarily structured theoretical inquiry into abnormal behavior, cognition, and affect. In a contemporary effort to both validate current diagnostic practices and bridge the gap between clinical diagnosis and the basic science of psychopathology, the National Institute of Mental Health has recently developed a Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) as an alternative approach to classification (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). The RDoC is aimed at identifying transdiagnostic biological and psychological processes by breaking down traditional diagnostic constructs into their underlying elements.

Classification must also be understood in a broader sociopolitical and institutional context.

Diagnostic systems are not value-neutral constructs, but are embedded in a network of institutional, professional, and economic interests (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). Although classification can have socially beneficial functions (e.g., helping to validate people’s experience and facilitating access to treatment and care), it can also play a role in stigma, discrimination, and social marginalization. Critics have also argued that, to some extent, diagnostic practice is an exercise in power relationships, as it can be used to define deviance in a manner that privileges the values of dominant groups in a society.
