Cultural Concepts of Distress

As you learned earlier in the module, DSM-5-TR promotes a more nuanced approach to the issues of culture and psychopathology using the notion of Cultural Concepts of Distress (CCD). The latter replaced the outdated construct of culture-bound syndromes that was widely used in DSM-IV and earlier versions of the manual. There was a concern with the term culture-bound syndromes that it exoticized non-Western populations’ experience of distress, implying that only some populations and their distress are culture-bound. In contrast to that, the more inclusive and dynamic construct of CCD was developed to highlight that all people’s experience of distress—including symptoms experience, naming, meaning, and communication—is culturally constructed and shaped (APA, 2022; Kaiser, 2019; Karthick & Barwa, 2017).


The DSM-5-TR describes at least three related but distinct forms of CCDs. First, cultural syndromes, which are local recognized and labeled clusters of symptoms that often co-occur in given cultural groups and are perceived as a “culturally coherent pattern of experiences, whether or not the group understands them as an illness” (APA, 2022, p. 840). Second, cultural idioms of distress are shared linguistic or behavioral expressions (somatization, metaphors, ways of crying or laughing) through which individuals may express their suffering without necessarily communicating about a distinct psychiatric disorder (Kaiser, 2019; Wahid et al., 2022). Third, cultural explanations or perceived causes are related to culturally derived models of illness etiology, which often ascribe a particular form of distress to a spiritual imbalance, interpersonal disagreement, social adversity, or a sin/violation of a moral norm (APA, 2022; Kaiser, 2019).

In combination, all of these emphasize the need for cultural humility and the understanding that, although similar phenomenological experiences can occur across groups, their meanings could be strikingly different with important implications for assessment, diagnosis, and treatment planning (Bennett, 2023; Karthick & Barwa, 2017; Moleiro, 2018).

To Illustrate

For example, neurasthenia is an example of a cultural syndrome that might be translated to the DSM diagnostic criteria as a combination of depression, anxiety, and trauma-related disorders in different contexts. In fact, global mental health research has shown that the relationship between CCDs and DSM-defined disorders can be very different in different contexts.

Recent meta-analytic research has shown a high degree of heterogeneity in empirical investigations on mapping cultural syndromes to standard diagnostic categories of anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder. Based on this meta-analysis, the authors argued that attempts to directly equate cultural syndromes to Western diagnoses might result in conceptual distortion and strong reductionism, therefore, these relationships need to be further investigated through methodologically more rigorous and culturally sensitive research (Kohrt et al., 2014). In other words, current scholarship has moved away from an initial interest in CCDs as local approximations of DSM-defined disorders (Brett-Green et al., 2007). The CCDs have been repositioned more as a complementary perspective that could capture the patients’ distress not addressed in the DSM categories. Clinically, CCDs could be used to avoid checklist-based diagnosis and to make more efforts in engaging with patients, such as by using DSM-5-TR Cultural Formulation Interview. This may lead to recognizing culturally normative ways of distress, avoiding misdiagnosis, and agreeing on treatment planning in a more collaborative way with the patient (Loannou et al., 2023). On an ethical and epistemological level, CCDs challenge the clinicians and researchers to reflect on whose experience the diagnostic categories represent and recognize culture as a factor that is shaping psychiatry knowledge.

Clinical Examples of Cultural Concepts of Distress

Ataques de Nervios

In some Caribbean and Latin American contexts, a person experiencing ataques de nervios might describe severe emotional upset, crying, trembling, shouting, dissociation, feelings of heat in the chest or head, aggression or suicidal gestures (Roche-Miranda et al., 2023). Triggers for an episode can include a major interpersonal stressor, such as an argument, a loss, or a broken relationship.

Ataques de nervios are not seen as a chronic disorder in Caribbean and Latin American culture but as a socially recognized way of expressing intense stress (Roche-Miranda et al., 2023).

Diagnostically, ataques de nervios could present as panic disorder, a dissociative disorder, or a trauma- or stressor-related disorder. Viewing an attack in isolation within a Western diagnostic framework would not account for the episodic, non-pathological nature of the experience in its cultural context. A culturally informed, CCD-based evaluation would consider the context of the episode.

Spirit Possession or Dissociative Trance States

Some clients may present with altered state of consciousness, amnesia, change in voice or behavior, and other experiences described as being due to an external spiritual force (e.g., Bovey et al., 2025; Bhavsar et al., 2016).

In certain cultural and religious contexts, possession trance states are normative, ritualized, and socially sanctioned phenomena, rather than psychiatric symptoms.

Clinicians should be able to distinguish between culturally normative trance states and dissociative disorders based on the volition, distress, impairment, and cultural context. In this case, there is a risk of misdiagnosis due to lack of cultural literacy.
