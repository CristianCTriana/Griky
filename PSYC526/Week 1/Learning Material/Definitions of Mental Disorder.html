Definitions of Mental Disorder

A common and seemingly intractable issue in psychopathology has to do with how to define mental disorder. The first few editions of the DSM as well as the ICD did not include an explicit definition of the term “mental disorder.” It was not until the publication of the DSM–III and Robert Spitzer’s leadership that a first attempt to provide a systematic definition was made. Spitzer’s influential definition of mental disorder as a kind of medical disorder was widely criticized—in part because it appeared to place an undue emphasis on psychiatry to the exclusion of other mental health disciplines such as psychology and social work (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). Spitzer and others responded to these criticisms by explicitly stating that mental disorders should be construed as medical disorders that, by their primary symptoms, were psychological or behavioral as opposed to somatic.


Wakefield (1993) has offered a different influential definition of mental disorder, defining disorders as “harmful dysfunctions.” In this view, disorders result from the failure of internal mechanisms to perform their evolutionarily selected functions, and this failure leads to harm or injury. Wakefield and others have argued that a proper definition of mental disorder requires both value-laden and science-laden elements (e.g., a value-laden element, such as distress or harm, and a science-laden element, such as dysfunction). In this sense, current editions of the DSM are likely to include distress, impairment, or clinical significance either within diagnostic definitions or as part of the criteria thresholds used to make diagnoses (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). In spite of these changes, much controversy and disagreement remain about the proper scope and nature of mental disorder.

In DSM-5-TR, mental disorders are defined as syndromes (i.e., clusters of symptoms) that reflect clinically significant disturbances in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior. They indicate dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning (APA, 2022). Disturbances associated with a mental disorder are typically associated with substantial distress or impairment in social or occupational functioning or other areas of important activities. 

It is Critical to Note

That responses to stressors that are normative or expectable within a cultural context are explicitly excluded from diagnostic consideration. In this regard, there is an explicit expectation that assessment and diagnosis should be conducted in a culturally informed and contextually sensitive manner.

The ICD, 11th Edition, published by the World Health Organization, defines mental disorders within a larger, global context of a classification system for all health conditions. ICD-11 is strongly focused on clinical utility in diverse cultural and healthcare settings and is increasingly dimensional in its orientation (e.g., dimensional severity specifiers for all disorders, as well as dimensionality in the revised model for personality disorders). This reflects a broader shift from purely categorical to hybrid and dimensional approaches to defining psychopathology as existing along continua, rather than entirely distinct diagnostic categories.
