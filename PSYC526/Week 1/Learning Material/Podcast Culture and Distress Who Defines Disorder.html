Transcript: Culture and Distress: Who Defines “Disorder”?

INTRO (0:00–0:45)

When someone cries uncontrollably during a family conflict, collapses under stress, hears a voice others don’t hear, or describes emotional pain through physical symptoms, our instinct in psychology is often to ask: What disorder is this?

But today, I want to pause that instinct and ask a different question:

Who gets to decide what counts as a disorder in the first place?

That question sits at the center of this course—and it’s especially important as we begin our exploration of psychopathology.

PART 1: WHAT DO WE MEAN BY “DISORDER”? (0:45–2:30)

In psychology and psychiatry, we rely on classification systems like the DSM‑5‑TR and the ICD‑11 to help us describe and organize mental health conditions. These systems give us shared language. They guide research. They influence treatment decisions, insurance coverage, and access to care.

But classification systems are tools, not neutral truths.

Both the DSM and ICD define mental disorders as patterns of distress or impairment that are clinically significant. That sounds straightforward—until we ask a crucial follow‑up question:

Distressing to whom? Impairing according to which standards?

What counts as distress, dysfunction, or impairment is never evaluated in a vacuum. It is always interpreted through cultural values, expectations, and norms about emotion, behavior, and personhood.

So while diagnostic systems aim for reliability and consistency, they are inevitably shaped by the cultural contexts in which they are created and applied.

PART 2: CULTURAL CONCEPTS OF DISTRESS (2:30–4:30)

This is where the idea of cultural concepts of distress becomes essential.

Across cultures, people experience and express psychological suffering in different ways. Some communities emphasize emotional expression. Others value restraint. Some frame distress primarily as physical pain or fatigue. Others understand distress through spiritual, relational, or moral explanations.

These expressions of distress are not random—and they are not necessarily pathological.

For example, in some cultural contexts, intense emotional reactions during interpersonal conflict are understood as expected responses to relational rupture, not signs of emotional dysregulation. In others, distress may be communicated through headaches, stomach pain, or exhaustion rather than sadness or worry.

In still other contexts, hearing voices or sensing spiritual presences may be culturally meaningful experiences rather than immediate indicators of psychosis.

The key point here is simple but powerful:

Expression does not automatically equal disorder.

When we ignore cultural context, we risk misunderstanding what a person is communicating about their suffering.

PART 3: SHIFTING DEFINITIONS OF “NORMALITY” (4:30–6:30)

One of the most important insights from cultural psychology is that normality is not universal.

Behaviors that are considered unusual, excessive, or even disordered in one cultural setting may be typical—or even valued—in another. Emotional expressiveness, independence, deference, spirituality, and coping styles all exist on culturally shaped continuums.

This matters because clinicians—and students of psychopathology—are constantly making judgments about what is “normal enough” versus what requires intervention.

Without cultural awareness, two major risks emerge.

The first is over‑pathologizing: labeling culturally normative behaviors as symptoms simply because they don’t match dominant expectations.

The second is under‑recognizing distress: failing to identify real suffering because it doesn’t look like the forms emphasized in Western diagnostic models.

Both errors have consequences—for individuals, families, and communities.

PART 4: CULTURAL HUMILITY VS. CULTURAL CHECKLISTS (6:30–7:45)

At this point, it might be tempting to think that cultural competence means memorizing lists of cultural “traits” or rules.

But culture doesn’t work that way.

That’s why contemporary psychology emphasizes cultural humility rather than cultural expertise.

Cultural humility involves ongoing self‑reflection, openness to learning, and a willingness to ask questions rather than make assumptions. Instead of asking, Which diagnosis fits this behavior? we ask:

What does this experience mean to the person?

How is it understood in their family or community?

Is it causing distress or impairment in context?

These questions don’t replace diagnosis—but they protect against misdiagnosis.

PART 5: CLINICAL AND ETHICAL IMPLICATIONS (7:45–8:45)

Diagnostic labels carry power.

They can open doors to treatment, support, and validation. But they can also bring stigma, misunderstanding, and exclusion—especially when applied without cultural context.

Ethically, clinicians and psychologists are responsible not just for accuracy, but for impact.

Mislabeling culturally normative distress as pathology can erode trust, reinforce stereotypes, and widen disparities in mental health care. At the same time, failing to recognize genuine suffering because it doesn’t fit familiar patterns can deny people the help they need.

This is why cultural formulation is not an “extra” step—it’s an ethical safeguard.

CLOSING REFLECTION (8:45–9:30)

As you move through this course, I invite you to keep returning to one central question:

Before we define a behavior as disordered, whose norms are we using—and what are the consequences of that decision?

Psychopathology is not just about identifying symptoms. It’s about understanding suffering in context—and responding with care, humility, and responsibility.
