The Role of Culture in Defining Normal and Abnormal

Culture has been defined as a cultural framework or lens that underlies values, beliefs, concepts, rules, communication practices, and symbolic systems, as well as making sense of and experiencing various aspects of life. Culture has a foundation that provides people with normative values and beliefs about what is appropriate, good, useful, true, and meaningful (Bennett, 2023; Walker et al., 2023). This system is communicated and shaped through preferred or typical modes of thinking, feeling, and socializing with others on a daily basis. Culture is made up of different constructs, such as the family, ceremonial/ritual, customs, language, and religion/spirituality (Aggarwal & Lewis-Fernandez, 2020).

Values are expressed

through preferred patterns of communication and styles of expression, such as talking or writing and culturally specific ways of showing emotions, attitudes, and ideas.

Additionally, culture is seen as a meaning-making framework that acts as a lens through which human behaviors are understood, explained, legitimized, or invalidated. In other words, culture influences and even constructs what is “normal” and what is “abnormal”; that is, culture plays a role in the construction of whether certain behaviors are considered adaptive and healthy, or maladaptive and disordered (Moleiro, 2018). This has the implication that behaviors that are considered symptomatic or disordered in one culture may be perceived as normal, meaningful, or even valued in another. For this reason, there has been an increasing call to consider psychopathology within a sociocultural context and as an embedded process, rather than an individual-level disorder or something that is solely biologically determined (Moleiro, 2018). For example, culture impacts the content and expression of symptoms, the meaning attributed to distress, the language used to talk about suffering, as well as help-seeking and healing pathways (Moleiro, 2018).

Culture can affect beliefs and explanatory models of the causes and remedies for psychological distress and determine whether distress is perceived in psychological, spiritual, relational, or somatic terms. In turn, these beliefs can affect help-seeking preferences for the type of support that one receives, such as from family members, religious or traditional healers, or mental health professionals, and even treatment preferences and expectations about the therapeutic process, such as the anticipated duration and outcome, authority of the clinician, or interventions that are considered acceptable and effective.

Diagnostic considerations have also sought to acknowledge the importance of understanding the individual’s cultural background to make sense of their symptom presentation. This includes the introduction of cultural formulation in the DSM-5-TR as a framework to contextualize the symptom presentation within an individual’s cultural background, as well as the acknowledgment of cultural concepts of distress as distinct phenomena that are not captured by formal diagnoses (APA, 2022). These concepts serve to differentiate between culturally normative expressions of distress and clinically significant psychopathology, with the goal of preventing misdiagnosis.

It is Worth Noting

Experiences that are culturally patterned, for example, somatic symptoms of depression or idiosyncratic culturally syndromes of distress, may not always fit with the prototypes of Western diagnostic categories, but are nonetheless valid forms of psychological suffering.

Acknowledging the influence of culture on symptom presentation and the meaning-making framework through which individuals interpret their experience directly challenges the longstanding universalist perspective of previous DSMs, which presumed that diagnostic criteria could be applied without any contextualization or interpretation to individuals across populations. For example, this more recent perspective in the DSM has been used to argue that diagnostic categories and constructs are not value-free but, in fact, represent the values and perspectives of historically dominant groups, especially those in the West and from individualistic backgrounds (Moleiro, 2018). The implication of this view is that without culture or contextual information, there is the possibility of over-pathologizing symptoms in people from marginalized populations, under-recognizing psychological distress, or even interpreting culturally sanctioned practices or values as symptoms or signs of a disorder.

Overall, there is value both in the clinical and epistemological sense in trying to situate psychopathology within cultural contexts. Clinically, a greater attention to cultural factors and patient backgrounds can improve diagnostic accuracy, facilitate culturally responsive treatment planning, and strengthen the therapeutic alliance, among other benefits (Bennett, 2023). On the other hand, from an epistemological standpoint, an awareness of the sociocultural underpinnings of psychopathology can raise more critical questions around how psychiatric knowledge and nosology is constructed, what norms are privileged and which ones are not, and how power differentials affect these processes (Krueger & Blaney, 2023; Moleiro, 2018). In this way, a consideration of culture has the potential to both improve the clinical practice of psychologists and social workers and align this practice with ethical commitments to justice, equity, inclusion, and respect for human diversity.

To me, 

Defining culture in the definition of psychopathology will contribute to the specific learning objective of being able to describe how cultural contexts can influence what is considered “normal” and what is “abnormal.” However, it also invites consideration of the social and historical processes that have contributed to the construction of this specific field of knowledge, as well as the development and updates to the DSM.
