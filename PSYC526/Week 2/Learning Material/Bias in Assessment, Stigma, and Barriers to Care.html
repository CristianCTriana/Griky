Assessment, Diagnosis, and Cultural Formulation

Bias in Assessment, Stigma, and Barriers to Care

Bias in assessment can occur at multiple levels, including measurement, clinical decision-making, and systemic or structural factors. Measurement bias is present when assessment tools are primarily developed and normed on members of dominant cultural groups, potentially leading to differential item functioning across different populations (Neal et al., 2024; Lozano-Ruiz et al., 2021). This can result in certain test items not adequately capturing culturally normative expressions of distress or over-pathologizing behaviors that are adaptive or normative in specific cultural contexts. Clinician bias, which can include both explicit attitudes and implicit stereotypes, can also influence assessment processes. This might manifest in the setting of diagnostic thresholds, interpretations of symptom severity, or judgments about risk, credibility, or treatment recommendations. Implicit bias among healthcare providers has been found to influence decisions related to diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment recommendations, and these biases can differentially affect members of marginalized groups (Grammoustianou et al., 2025).

Systemic or Structural Bias

This encompasses broader inequities within the healthcare system, including differential access to culturally and linguistically matched providers, availability of interpretation services, or organizational policies that may emphasize efficiency and throughput over contextualized and holistic assessment.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that efforts to mitigate clinician bias (implicit bias training, structured decision aids, changes to clinical care pathways) have some short-term effectiveness in changing clinician attitudes and decision-making, but these effects often dissipate over time without ongoing institutional supports and incentives (Grammoustianou et al., 2025). As a result, efforts to reduce bias in assessment require both multilevel and multicomponent approaches that include clinician education but also organizational-level strategies to diversify the mental health workforce, partner with community groups, and implement culturally adapted models of care.

Bias in assessment can be further exacerbated by stigma and structural barriers that shape help-seeking, symptom disclosure, and retention in treatment. Stigma and stereotypes about mental illness, including beliefs about shame, moral failing, spiritual causation, or family dishonor, can deter individuals from acknowledging distress or seeking formal mental health care (Ahad et al., 2023; Al-Juhani et al., 2024). Structural barriers like language differences, lack of health insurance, limited transportation options, and scarcity of culturally congruent services can disproportionately affect immigrant, refugee, and low-income populations. Past negative experiences with healthcare systems, including perceived discrimination or cultural invalidation, may also contribute to mistrust and deter engagement with care.

Stigma-reduction efforts have increasingly focused on multi-component interventions that work at individual, community, and policy levels (Clay et al., 2020; Girma et al., 2022).

These can include public education campaigns, contact-based interventions that humanize mental illness through narratives of lived experience, and policy changes that protect against discrimination and increase access to care. Systematic reviews of interventions to reduce stigma around mental illness have emphasized that the effectiveness of anti-stigma efforts is highly context-dependent and that anti-stigma messages and strategies must be adapted to local cultural meanings and social structures to be effective.

Clinically, anti-bias and anti-stigma efforts can take many concrete and ethically mandated forms. These include using non-pathologizing and culturally sensitive language when interacting with patients, explicitly exploring a patient's explanatory model for their distress, providing flexible pathways to care that may or may not involve the individual in primary concern (family members, traditional healers), and advocating for structural changes that address material and institutional barriers. From a training perspective, developing a continual awareness of one's own assumptions and positionality is one way to reduce bias and support accurate assessment. These efforts to mitigate bias and stigma in assessment and diagnosis reflect an overarching principle that accurate diagnosis and effective treatment are inseparable from cultural responsiveness, social justice, and health equity.
