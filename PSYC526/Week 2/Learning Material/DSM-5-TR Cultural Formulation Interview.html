Assessment, Diagnosis, and Cultural Formulation

DSM-5-TR Cultural Formulation Interview

Quick Read

The DSM-5-TR Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI) [webpage] is a semi-structured interview that was developed to systematically elicit patients’ cultural perspectives on illness, identity, and care in a way that can be reliably integrated into a diagnostic formulation.

The CFI provides specific prompts to explore how individuals make sense of their distress, the meanings they give to symptoms, and contextual and cultural factors that shape their experiences (Jarvis et al, 2020). The five main domains of the CFI include cultural identity, explanatory models of illness, psychosocial stressors and supports, perceived barriers to care, and preferences for treatment. The goal of the CFI is to center clients’ lived meanings of distress as an important complement to other DSM-5-TR criteria to help increase diagnostic accuracy, avoid cultural misdiagnosis, and improve therapeutic alliance (Jarvis et al, 2020).

The main CFI consists of 16 open-ended questions and two optional modules to help guide culturally focused clinical questions for specific patient populations (e.g., immigrants and refugees, children and adolescents, older adults, spirituality and religion) and an informant version that can be administered to a family member or caregiver (Aggarwal & Lewis-Fernandez, 2020). Although the first administration of the CFI may take 20–50 minutes, most clinicians comment that the richness of the information outweighs the time needed to complete it. The CFI often elicits important clinically relevant information not obtained through a standard diagnostic interview, such as cultural factors impacting symptom expression, culturally patterned idioms of distress, help-seeking pathways, stigma, and culturally specific risk or protective factors.

Research on the clinical utility of the CFI has found that using the CFI increased clinicians’ cultural understanding and impacted diagnostic formulation and treatment planning in clinically significant ways (Jarvis et al, 2020).

In qualitative studies, CFI-informed assessments changed or revised diagnostic impressions, risk level, or intervention strategies, with the greatest effect seen in culturally diverse clinical populations. Patients also reported that they felt better understood and more respected when clinicians explicitly explored their explanatory models and cultural contexts. These findings point to the CFI as an important means of improving engagement and alliance. However, despite these potential benefits, there is evidence that uptake of the CFI has been mixed in clinical settings, with common barriers including time limitations in high-volume clinical settings, clinician lack of training or confidence using culturally focused clinical tools, and uncertainty about the best way to use CFI findings to guide formal diagnostic assessment and treatment planning. Differential uptake across clinical settings also points to the importance of organizational culture and clinician supports, including supervision and role modeling, for successful implementation of the CFI. For this reason, more recent literature on CFI use has called for structured training and supervision, as well as using prompts to integrate CFI-derived information into electronic health records and multidisciplinary case discussions, to make cultural information meaningful in diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making (Aggarwal et al., 2020).

For graduate students, the CFI can also be used to frame a cultural humility and reflexivity skills learning and teaching framework. 

In this way, the CFI helps move the training of clinicians beyond surface-level ideas of cultural competence and towards an ongoing, patient-centered process of inquiry to understand how culture, context, and power affect mental health. The CFI therefore speaks to ethical and professional standards of being a culturally responsive, patient-centered clinician and highlights the importance of culture in mental illness assessment.
