Assessment, Diagnosis, and Cultural Formulation

Differential Diagnosis Considerations Across Cultural Groups

In terms of differential diagnosis, the clinician also needs to be able to recognize phenomenological differences in symptom expression across cultures from nosological differences. How a given culture orients around experiencing and expressing distress in affective, cognitive, behavioral, or somatic terms will have a strong effect on presentation style, which in turn influences the difficulty of differentiating between mood, anxiety, psychotic, and somatoform spectra (Patel & Hall, 2021).

For Instance

A trauma reaction may present to a clinician in other cultural contexts as somatic complaints or withdrawal, as opposed to the distressing, involuntary re-experiencing and memories more central to Western case definitions.

In such cases, diagnostic evaluations that rely on a Western prototype of traumatic stress symptoms will lead to erroneous underdiagnosis. Recent reviews have emphasized the need for differential diagnostic algorithms that are sensitive to local idioms of distress and can accommodate culture-specific syndromes, as well as common categories (Patel & Hall, 2021).

Help-seeking pathways and comorbidity profiles differ across cultural groups, so the base rates clinicians should be anticipating are also likely to differ, which in turn determines the positive predictive value of many screening measures (e.g., Yildiz et al., 2025). Clinicians need to make use of culture-sensitive probes (e.g., for spirit possession beliefs, for somatic idioms, for family level explanatory models) and be cautious about overgeneralizing or making inferences using Western diagnostic categories without triangulating cultural formulation data, collateral history, and functional impairment information.
