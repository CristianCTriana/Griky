Assessment, Diagnosis, and Cultural Formulation

Psychiatric Assessment and Diagnostic Interviewing

Sattler (2024) usefully summarized that psychological assessment is a systematic information-gathering process that ultimately develops a working picture of the individual being assessed, with the goal of informing evidence-based decision-making. A contemporary psychiatric assessment is an organized integration of history taking, symptom inquiry, mental status examination, risk assessment, and the interpretation of collateral information. Principal assessment goals include screening for potential problems, informing diagnostic formulation, aiding in intervention planning (counseling and rehabilitation), and tracking progress.

Good diagnostic interviewing involves striking a balance between open-ended narrative (eliciting the patient’s explanatory model and context) and focused, standardized probes to ensure that key domains of symptoms are evaluated reliably.

The qualitative information that is obtained through a well-conducted clinical interview is invaluable (in truth, essential) to developing an accurate picture of what is happening for the examinee (Sattler, 2024). Clinical interviews are used to obtain psychosocial and developmental history information in areas of presenting problem, family dynamics and constellation, medical history, developmental history, educational history, vocational history, relationship status, social history, trauma history, psychological history, legal history, and substance use history. The more complete the information, the more accurate the assessment interpretation piece can be. Recent best-practice reviews point to the value of transdiagnostic screening (to screen for comorbidity and syndromic overlap), structured clinical interviews when reliability is necessary, and collateral sources (family, primary care records) to reduce information gaps and diagnostic error (Neulinger et al., 2024).

These methods are particularly critical in graduate training because they emphasize clinical judgment and empirical rigor.

Beyond technique, interviewing requires reflexivity about clinician influence: interview style, question phrasing, and nonverbal behavior can shape symptom disclosure and the clinical picture. Interviews can be either completely unstructured (“free-form”), semi-structured (an outline is specified, but the wording and follow-up questions are left up to the interviewer), or structured (much more rigid in terms of using a standardized order and language; generally used in research studies as opposed to everyday clinical practice) (Sattler, 2024). Empirical work shows that diagnostic interviews perform better when clinicians are trained in both structured tools and culturally sensitive elicitation methods; thus, training programs are therefore encouraged to teach students how to alternate between semi-structured tools (for comparability) and patient-centered narrative (for validity), and to use audio/video feedback and standardized patient exercises to improve diagnostic skill (Bohman, 2023).
