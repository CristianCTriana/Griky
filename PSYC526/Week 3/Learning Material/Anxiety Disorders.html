Anxiety, Trauma, and Stressor-Related Disorders

Anxiety Disorders

Personality is strongly and clearly associated with anxiety disorders, and many theoretical models for the development of psychopathology have focused on the relationship between personality and mental disorders.

The vulnerability model is one such model that conceptualizes personality traits as risk factors for psychopathology (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).
In contrast, the common cause model sees anxiety disorders as extreme and pendent manifestations of pre-existing personality traits (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).
The scar model, on the other hand, conceptualizes psychopathology as the cause of long-lasting personality changes.
The last model to be considered is the pathoplasty model that sees personality as influencing the course, severity, and phenomenological expression of the disorder (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

Temperament, as a domain of personality, has been found to be linked to anxiety disorders as well. Neuroticism, for example, has been defined as a partially heritable tendency toward negative emotionality and is believed to be a transdiagnostic risk factor for anxiety disorders, as well as major depressive disorders. Several processes through which neuroticism might confer vulnerability for psychopathology have been put forward (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

The stably elevated negative effect hypothesis posits that people high in neuroticism have chronically elevated levels of negative affect that are stable across different contexts and are likely to cause increased symptom worsening.
In the stress amplification hypothesis, on the other hand, it is argued that neuroticism interacts with environmental stress to cause or exacerbate psychopathology.

Neuroticism has been shown to be cross-sectionally associated with internalizing disorders, including anxiety disorders.

Behavioral inhibition, defined as the response to novel or unfamiliar situations that is marked by shyness, withdrawal, avoidance, uneasiness, and fear, has also been implicated in the development of anxiety disorders (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

In addition, anxiety sensitivity has been identified as another risk factor for anxiety disorders. Anxiety sensitivity is the fear of anxiety-related sensations that are believed to cause physical illness, social embarrassment, or further psychological issues.

Anxiety disorders are one of the most common psychiatric disorders, which are characterized by excessive fear and worry, physiological hyperarousal, and avoidance (APA, 2022).

Generalized Anxiety Disorder is defined by chronic, excessive, and uncontrollable worry about different life circumstances and problems and is accompanied by other symptoms such as restlessness, sleep problems, and difficulty in concentrating (APA, 2022).
Panic Disorder is defined by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks followed by maladaptive changes in behavior (e.g., fear of panic attacks) or worries about the consequences of panic attacks (APA, 2022).
Specific phobias are defined by an extreme and disproportional fear or anxiety of a specific object or situation, which is clinically significant and causes distress or impairment in one’s life (APA, 2022).

Neurobiological models of anxiety disorders have focused on reduced white matter integrity (as indicated by lower fractional anisotropy), alterations in fear-related circuits (e.g., amygdala, prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex), and neurotransmitter dysregulation—particularly GABA and serotonin (e.g., Glenn et al., 2022). Anxiety-related psychopathology has been found to be both heritable and environmentally influenced, with genetic and environmental factors likely interacting with each other to contribute to the development of anxiety disorders.

Although it has been established that the DSM anxiety disorder diagnoses are distinct categorical entities, an alternative view is that anxiety disorders are subtypes of a larger anxiety syndrome that are different only in a descriptive sense, that is, the content of the fear and worry is different (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). This dimensional view may or may not be correct, but both models have theoretical support, and the existing evidence suggests significant overlap in both the etiological processes and processes that maintain generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, and specific phobias. As such, there is much to be gained by viewing these disorders as a group and examining risk factors and maintaining processes that are common across these disorders.

Evidence from cross-cultural studies has consistently shown that anxiety disorders are universal and are found in all societies.

However, the prevalence, course, and even recognition of these conditions are greatly influenced by culture. The ways that people label and talk about their anxiety-related experiences, and the types of anxiety experiences they notice and describe, are shaped by cultural explanatory models, norms, and belief systems. Therefore, even though risk and vulnerability factors are likely to be similar across cultures, the types of symptoms that people develop, the degree to which people are distressed by their symptoms, and help-seeking behavior are likely to be affected by culture.

Cultural models of illness, as well as beliefs about the nature of mental illness, expression of emotions, and other culturally sanctioned forms of idiom of distress can all contribute to variations in how anxiety symptoms are framed, whether they are seen as psychological or somatic, spiritual, or social (e.g., Glenn et al., 2022; Javaid et al., 2023; Moreno-Montero et al., 2025). For example, excessive worrying and fear of the future are two common cognitive symptoms of anxiety. However, in more collectivistic or non-Western cultures, individuals are more likely to report somatic symptoms (such as dizziness, gastrointestinal complaints, headaches, and chest discomfort) than cognitive symptoms. In contrast, Western individuals are more likely to report cognitive symptoms. Western emphasis on the psychological over the physical is thus likely to influence the reporting of anxiety symptoms.

In other cultures, high levels of anxiety and distress might be normalized, and verbal expression of internal emotional states might be actively discouraged, making it less likely that anxiety symptoms are recognized and diagnosed. The higher thresholds that people have for reporting distress are an additional way in which culture influences whether anxiety is identified as a problem and whether people seek care.

Cross-cultural evidence has also pointed to the existence of culture-bound syndromes (e.g., ataques de nervios, spirit possession [Week 1]) that are culturally linked to anxiety but are not part of the Western understanding of anxiety (e.g., Glenn et al., 2022). These syndromes, as well as many symptoms of anxiety, have at their core fear, autonomic hyperarousal, and avoidance but can look very different from each other in different cultural contexts. 

For Example

Some cultures might not recognize cognitive symptoms, such as worrying, as indicative of a psychological issue and instead frame them as normal or somatic in nature. This can challenge the strictly categorical approach to the diagnosis of psychopathology and suggests that more dimensional and culturally informed frameworks are needed for the assessment and diagnosis of anxiety and other types of mental illness (e.g., Bovey et al., 2025; Bhavsar et al., 2016; Roche-Miranda et al., 2023). The cultural differences in the experience of anxiety can therefore have implications for assessment, diagnosis, and treatment.

The cross-cultural evidence presented here illustrates that, for a culturally diverse group of people, clinicians must be able to conceptualize anxiety in a way that is both accurate and respectful of their cultural background. Clinicians must therefore be aware of how culture influences symptom expression, the use of language, attention, and beliefs about the mind and body, as well as migration and sociopolitical histories when working with people with anxiety disorders. In this way, cultural formulation can be used to enrich diagnostic practice, make it more effective and unbiased, and improve outcomes for people across all cultural backgrounds.
