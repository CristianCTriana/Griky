Anxiety, Trauma, and Stressor-Related Disorders

Contextualized Trauma: Displacement, Immigration Stress, Historical Trauma

Trauma and stress responses are best contextualized within wider sociohistorical, political, and ecological factors rather than being pathologized at the level of individual psychopathology. Displacement-related stress and immigration stress are significant and often chronic forms of trauma that have a substantial impact on mental health across the lifespan. Displaced individuals and immigrants who have faced adverse circumstances during their migration process are likely to experience cumulative stress exposure and trauma from pre-migration experiences (e.g., war, persecution, poverty), migration itself (e.g., dangerous journeys, family separation), and post-migration stressors (e.g., acculturative stress, language barriers, discrimination, legal insecurity, socioeconomic marginalization) (Villasenor-Ingersoll, 2024). This cumulative stress exposure places these individuals at high risk for developing and maintaining anxiety disorders, trauma-related disorders, and somatic symptomatology.

Ulysses Syndrome or the immigrant syndrome of chronic and multiple stress has been suggested to describe the set of psychological, emotional, and somatic symptoms that can occur as a result of the migration experience when individuals are subjected to extreme and chronic stressors (Villasenor-Ingersoll, 2024).

This approach importantly underscores that such responses are not necessarily indicative of a primary psychiatric disorder but rather can be an understandable response to continued adversity, uncertainty, and loss. Some of the common symptoms associated with Ulysses syndrome include anxiety, depressive symptoms, feelings of exhaustion, sleep disturbances, and physical complaints (Villasenor-Ingersoll, 2024). These symptoms illustrate the limitations of using traditional diagnostic categories in cases where there is a lack of consideration of individuals’ stress exposure and structural vulnerability.

Historical trauma is another example of how trauma can be contextualized at the collective and intergenerational level. Historical trauma has been used to describe the emotional and psychological wounding over the lifespan and across generations resulting from massive group trauma (e.g., colonization, genocide, slavery, forced removal, cultural erasure) (e.g., Gameon & Skewes, 2019; Sevillano et al., 2022; Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018). Studies have found that there is an association between historical trauma exposure and increased rates of PTSD, anxiety, substance use, and stress-related physical health conditions among Indigenous peoples and other historically oppressed populations (e.g., Gameon & Skewes, 2019; Sevillano et al., 2022; Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018). It is important to note that the effects of historical trauma are not only related to past events but are also perpetuated by continued social and structural inequities including systemic discrimination, economic inequities, and limited access to culturally responsive care.

Trauma and stress-related vulnerability across the lifespan can be transmitted across generations through multiple pathways.

Some of these mechanisms include disruption in caregiving and attachment, social learning of threat and mistrust, chronic stress and marginalization, and recent evidence in epigenetic modifications in stress response systems (e.g., Gameon & Skewes, 2019; Sevillano et al., 2022; Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018). Developmental timing may be a key factor in intergenerational trauma transmission such that when trauma or chronic stress occurs during sensitive developmental periods in childhood and adolescence, it may cause changes to neurobiological stress systems, which can increase vulnerability throughout life. At the same time, cultural strengths, collective meaning-making, and community cohesion are often a source of resilience and healing.

Modern scholarship on trauma increasingly advocates for frameworks that integrate contextual, cultural, historical, and structural factors rather than purely focusing on individual-level symptoms and dysregulation. Such an approach would take into account the importance of differentiating between pathological trauma responses and culturally normative and understandable reactions to systemic oppression, displacement, and collective loss. From a clinical perspective, this underscores the importance of engaging in culturally responsive, trauma-informed assessment and interventions that are ethically sound. Clinically, this approach can help with validating individuals’ experiences, attend to sociopolitical context, and engage with community-based sources of resilience.
