Mood Disorders, Suicide, and Cultural Determinants of Emotion

Bipolar Spectrum Disorders

Bipolar spectrum disorders (BPS) refer to a group of mood disorders that manifest as recurring episodes of mood, energy, activity, and cognition disturbances, and have variable polarity across depressive and manic or hypomanic poles. Bipolar spectrum disorders include bipolar I disorder, bipolar II disorder, and related disorders such as cyclothymic disorder and other specified bipolar and related disorders, which include subthreshold or atypical presentations (APA, 2022). While these conditions differ in symptom severity and diagnostic thresholds, they are all typified by similar core features of episodicity, mood lability, and marked functional impairment, which frequently disrupt interpersonal and occupational functioning, and quality of life.

Bipolar disorders are characterized by an episodic and recurrent clinical course, with mood episodes often alternating with periods of partial or full remission (APA, 2022).

However, growing evidence suggests that many patients experience residual subsyndromal depressive and anxiety symptoms, which persist across mood episodes. These chronic residual symptoms can lead to significant functional impairment and increased relapse risk and also challenge the notion of bipolar disorder as a disorder defined only by discrete episodes of mood symptoms. Bipolar II disorder may often be misdiagnosed or underrecognized due to the relative subtlety of hypomanic symptoms and greater number of depressive episodes over the course of the illness.

Etiological models of bipolar spectrum disorders suggest a large genetic component, with heritability estimates among the highest of any psychiatric disorder and estimates of over 70% being commonly reported in twin studies. This genetic vulnerability is proposed to interact with a variety of neurobiological systems that are dysregulated in bipolar disorders, including those related to circadian regulation, reward sensitivity, and emotion regulation. Support for fronto-limbic dysfunction in bipolar spectrum disorders comes from neuroimaging and neurocognitive studies, which have reported reduced prefrontal control over limbic regions that are involved in processing emotional salience and reward sensitivity, among others (O’Connell et al., 2018; Schwarz et al., 2020). Dysregulation of dopaminergic pathways have been implicated in manic and hypomanic symptoms, whereas depressive symptoms have been more consistently linked to serotonergic and glutamatergic dysfunction, as well as impaired neuroplasticity.

Psychological and behavioral models have similarly been developed, which focus on common features of vulnerability to disruption in emotion regulation, increased reward reactivity, and loss of control in goal pursuit.

Notably, some patients with bipolar spectrum disorders appear to have increased sensitivity to reward cues and goal attainment, which can trigger hypomanic or manic episodes in response to success, increased goal striving, or sleep loss. Stressful life events, loss, or other circadian disturbances can also precipitate depressive episodes. These findings have been integrated into diathesis–stress and stress–sensitization models, in which repeated mood episodes lead to increased neurobiological and psychological reactivity to increasingly lower levels of stress over time (O’Connell et al., 2018; Schwarz et al., 2020).

Clinically, bipolar spectrum disorders most frequently begin in adolescence or early adulthood, with earlier age of onset associated with more severe illness course, increased comorbidity, and more marked functional impairment. Diagnosis of BPS can often be delayed, particularly for bipolar II disorder, which is associated with greater exposure to antidepressant monotherapy and potential worsening of mood instability or induction of manic symptoms in treatment. Comorbidity with other psychiatric conditions is the rule, rather than the exception, with high rates of co-occurring anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and personality pathology that can further complicate clinical presentation and management.

Social and cultural factors have been found to influence the presentation, recognition, and diagnosis of bipolar spectrum disorders. Cross-cultural studies have shown differences in the interpretation and perception of manic and hypomanic symptoms, which are likely to be influenced by cultural norms and values regarding emotional expressiveness, productivity, spirituality, and social behavior (Latifan et al., 2023; Ntumi et al., 2025; O’Connell et al., 2018; Tamir et al., 2024).

For Instance

Elevated energy, decreased need for sleep, or an expansive mood may be more accepted or even valued in certain sociocultural contexts, leading to underdiagnosis of hypomania. 

Alternatively, unusual or culturally unfamiliar expressions of grandiosity, religiosity, or irritability may be inappropriately pathologized. These cultural differences may impact epidemiologic estimates, clinician interpretation, and misdiagnosis patterns across different cultural groups (Fullerton & Tesfay, 2025). Structural factors such as access to mental health services, stigma, and culturally congruent explanatory models can also impact illness course and engagement with care.

Epidemiologic studies have consistently found high risk of suicide and suicide-related outcomes in bipolar spectrum disorders, with risk exceeding that of unipolar depressive disorders. In longitudinal cohort studies, patients with bipolar disorder have been found to have a lifetime risk of suicide that is 20–30 times greater than the general population. Suicide risk is greatest during depressive and mixed states, as well as early in the course of illness and during periods of functional decline. Bipolar diagnosis, younger age of onset, longer duration of depressive episodes, rapid cycling, comorbid substance use, and poorer psychosocial functioning have been reported as independent predictors of suicidal behavior (Miola et al., 2023). This high risk highlights the clinical importance of a thorough suicide risk assessment and close monitoring during transition periods, as well as interventions that target depressive symptoms and functional impairment, rather than manic symptoms only.
