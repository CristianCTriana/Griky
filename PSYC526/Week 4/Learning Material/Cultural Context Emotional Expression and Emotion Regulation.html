Mood Disorders, Suicide, and Cultural Determinants of Emotion

Cultural Context: Emotional Expression and Emotion Regulation

The intense crying after listening to these taunts (cryogenic therapy) was the first technique I heard about related to emotion regulation (ER). I see it differently today, for various reasons: (a) ER is not entirely an intrapsychic process. In fact, there is evidence to suggest that emotion is a cultural artifact (Tamir et al., 2024). As a result, the way people experience, express, and make sense of psychological distress is also influenced by culture; (b) evidence has found that culturally normative values (e.g., individualism-collectivism, power distance, social interdependence) are moderators of the utilization and maladaptive psychological consequences of the core ER strategies of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression.

In some cultures, suppression is the norm and is not maladaptive, while in others, it is a significant predictor of poorer mental health, and such differences mandate a culturally sensitive approach to the concept (Tamir et al., 2024); (c) culture also affects help-seeking behaviors and stigmatization, e.g., a study done on university students in Ghana showed that their cultural norms heightened the stigmatization associated with help-seeking, leading to low rates of formal help-seeking unless digital engagement moderated it (Ntumi et al., 2025); (d) in addition to that, there are also other culture-related stressors that have been studied and found to be associated with different variables. For instance, acculturative stress, perceived discrimination, and ethnic identity have been investigated, and the results show that these culturally based stressors affected depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation in ethnically diverse emerging adults, with these variables (ethnic identity, for example) serving as either a risk or protective factor (Polanco-Roman et al., 2024).

In Short

From this literature, the clinician’s implication is that there is a need for cultural competence, which will help in a correct assessment of the ER processes as well as in tailoring a suitable culturally grounded intervention for the individual.

As noted at the outset, there is evidence to suggest that people from different cultures are differentially vulnerable to depressive disorders (Fullerton & Tesfaye, 2025; Latifan et al., 2023). It is also known that depression is not an entity found in isolation, at the intrapsychic level. The characteristics of a given society and its sociocultural, contextual, and structural dynamics could shape the expression, interpretation, and clinical recognition of depression in people living in this society. For example, they may shape the way people experience, label, talk about, and respond to distress in their own bodies or that of others in their family, community, or health care system (Feliciano et al., 2024). As a result, there may be relevant clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic implications depending on the individual’s cultural group membership (Latifan et al., 2023). At this point, it is important to highlight some of the common cross-cultural variations in the manifestation of depressive disorders and discuss their implications for assessment, diagnosis, and treatment.

The one variation that has been the most thoroughly documented concerns the symptomatic expression of depression in different cultural contexts. As noted previously, in most non-Western and collectivistic cultural groups, depressive disorders are less likely to be expressed through self-reported sadness, guilt, hopelessness, and anhedonia (Fullerton & Tesfaye, 2025; O’Connell et al., 2018; Schwarz et al., 2020). Instead, there is an overrepresentation of physical or somatic symptoms, such as fatigue, headache, gastrointestinal distress, or nonspecific bodily pain. It is likely that somatic complaints serve as a culturally sanctioned idiom of distress, allowing individuals to express their suffering without incurring social stigma or offense or disrupting social harmony. As a result, such patients may not receive the needed mental health care, thus leading to misdiagnosis, underdiagnosis, or medicalization of a potentially treatable psychological condition if cultural factors are not considered.

Cultural groups may also differ in emotional language and, by extension, the process of meaning making related to depression.

For Instance

Emotional states such as sadness, despair, guilt, or shame may be conceptualized and talked about in ways that emphasize their moral, spiritual, or interpersonal significance rather than a purely internal psychological experience (Polanco-Roman et al., 2024).

As a result, patients may ascribe their depressive symptoms to spiritual imbalance, bad luck, relationship problems, or failure to meet societal expectations rather than a mental disorder as such. These cultural explanatory models may also shape the individuals’ assumptions as to whether their condition is or is not an indication for professional mental health services, informal social support, or spiritual guidance. A clinician who does not take the time to understand the culturally specific explanatory models may elicit the patients’ resistance, disengagement, or treatment noncompliance.

As seen in the module, contextual stressors have been linked to both MDD and PDD, including in marginalized or historically oppressed populations (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). For example, chronic exposure to structural stressors (e.g., poverty, discrimination, immigration-related stressors, acculturative stress and strain, healthcare disparities, etc.) is associated with an increased risk for MDD and PDD (Krueger & Blaney, 2023). In individuals and communities who experience a history of chronic systemic adversity, depressive symptoms may be an adaptive response to ongoing stress rather than an episodic intrapsychic pathology. Similarly, PDD may result from exposure to sustained environmental stressors, such as chronic low mood, hopelessness, and anhedonia that result from environmental adversities.

Historical and intergenerational trauma is also a factor in culturally patterned risk for depression (Gameon & Skewes, 2019; Sevillano et al., 2022; Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018 [refer to Week 3]).

Communities affected by colonization, forced migration, slavery, or cultural oppression may have higher rates of depression than other cultural groups, with these symptoms shaped by historical and ongoing social inequities (e.g., Gameon & Skewes, 2019; Sevillano et al., 2022; Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018 [refer to week 3]). In turn, intergenerational transmission of trauma may occur through disrupted attachment, parenting stress, and intergenerational poverty or marginalization. The effect of these factors is higher risk for early onset and chronic depressive symptoms. It also challenges purely intrapsychic or individualistic models of depression and suggests the need to situate individual symptoms in their historical and sociocultural context.

Cultural factors may also impact stigma and help-seeking behaviors, which in turn affect illness course and outcome (Ahad et al., 2023; Latifan et al., 2023; Ntumi et al., 2025). In many cultural groups, depression is highly stigmatized, especially when it is considered a sign of weakness, lack of self-control, character flaw, or family disgrace. This, in turn, may delay treatment, contribute to chronicity and symptom burden, and increase suicide risk. Family and community support, in turn, may be protective or increase risk depending on their strength and cultural congruence.

All these factors also point to the need for a culturally responsive assessment and diagnosis of MDD and PDD. 

It requires that a clinician move beyond a symptom checklist and engage in a careful cultural exploration of the client, including cultural identity, migration history, language preferences, spiritual/religious orientation, and social context. The DSM-5-TR offers a Cultural Formulation Interview as a structured set of questions to clinicians who wish to understand the person in cultural context (APA, 2022). It elicits the person’s culturally specific understanding of the problem (distress, discomfort, symptoms, and so on), causes, coping strategies, and barriers to care, as well as a clinical decision whether these constructs are related to the patient’s clinical presentation. Such approaches can improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic alliance, especially in diverse clinical settings.

In turn, a culturally informed view of depression may lead to different treatment plans and prognostic expectations. For instance, psychotherapy that is culturally informed of the client’s core values, relational orientations, and meaning-making frameworks (such as CBTs that are culturally adapted or interventions based in community resources and networks) show improved outcomes and treatment engagement. In addition, a contextual focus on environmental stressors may also enable the clinician to adopt a more flexible, strength-based approach that can distinguish between individual pathology and environmentally driven distress, especially in PDD.
