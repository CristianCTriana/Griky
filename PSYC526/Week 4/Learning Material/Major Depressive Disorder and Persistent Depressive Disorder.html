Mood Disorders, Suicide, and Cultural Determinants of Emotion

Major Depressive Disorder and Persistent Depressive Disorder

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and persistent depressive disorder (PDD; previously known as dysthymia) are considered the “purest” forms of depression. Major depressive disorder is an episodic mood disorder that is diagnosed when a person has two or more weeks of depressed mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure along with other cognitive, emotional, and neurovegetative symptoms. These symptoms must represent a change from previous functioning and cause significant distress or impairment in interpersonal, occupational, and academic or role functioning. On the other hand, persistent depressive disorder is diagnosed when a person has a chronically depressed mood that is present for more days than not for at least two years (one year for children/adolescents). While PDD may have less severe neurovegetative symptoms than MDD, it often has a greater overall functional impact. The symptoms associated with PDD become more “chronic” or “part of one’s identity” (APA, 2022). It has also been noted that people with PDD often describe a depressed mood that has been present for “as long as they can remember” (APA, 2022).

Comorbidity between depressive disorders and anxiety disorders is relatively common and presents an additive and sometimes exponential degree of distress to the client. Substance use disorders, personality disorders, bipolar disorder, and other mood disorders are also common comorbid conditions with depressive disorders.

Potential etiological contributors to depressive disorders include biological, genetic, developmental, psychological, interpersonal, social, and cultural factors. Genetic and twin studies have indicated that there is a heritability to depressive disorders of about 30–40% for MDD (APA, 2022). Depression has a strong genetic component but is not caused by a specific gene or gene mutation. The neurobiological model of depression has been updated over the years, moving away from “monoamine deficiency theories” to include multiple neurobiological systems, including serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, HPA-axis upregulation, neuroplasticity, and inflammatory processes. Findings have also shown high proinflammatory cytokine activity and lower brain-derived neurotrophic factor in depressive disorders, especially treatment-resistant depression.

Depressive disorders can also be explained by various psychological theories, such as Beck’s cognitive theory. In Beck’s cognitive theory, core beliefs and negative cognitive schemas about the self, the world, and the future (collectively known as the cognitive triad) influence how people process information, leading to biased and pessimistic thinking (Beck et al., 1979). Negative cognitive biases in these three areas can lead to and maintain a depressed mood and behavior. Cognitive factors interact with other diatheses and stressors to increase vulnerability to depression. Depressive disorders also have an interpersonal component and have been explained by models of interpersonal dysfunction, including attachment issues, excessive reassurance seeking, and maladaptive interpersonal patterns. These theories support the interpersonal dysfunction in major depression and the role it plays in maintaining the disorder, particularly PDD.

In the Context of Diathesis–Stress Models

Depressive disorders are the result of the interplay between genetic, biological, and psychological vulnerabilities and environmental stressors.

These stressors can include early life trauma and neglect, which have been linked to a higher risk of earlier onset, chronicity, and treatment resistance in depressive disorders, in part due to long-lasting effects on stress-response systems and emotion regulation. Social support and other protective factors play a crucial role in mitigating the impact of stress and reducing the risk of onset and recurrence of depressive episodes. Addressing psychosocial and cultural risk and protective factors is important in the prevention and treatment of depressive disorders.

Depressive disorders have a significant negative impact on psychosocial, interpersonal, and occupational functioning and are a leading cause of disability worldwide. Depression not only affects a person’s ability to perform daily tasks and engage in social and work activities but also increases the risk of medical comorbidities and early mortality. One of the most important clinical implications of depressive disorders is their strong association with suicidal ideation and behavior. Factors such as symptom severity, hopelessness, and comorbid anxiety or substance use can increase the risk of suicide. Research has shown that up to 60% of people with mood disorders experience suicidal ideation in their lifetime, and a significant proportion will attempt suicide or die by suicide at some point (Khan et al., 2025). This highlights the need for continuous suicide risk assessment, even during periods of symptom remission, and the ethical and clinical importance of early identification and long-term intervention and prevention in depressive disorders.
