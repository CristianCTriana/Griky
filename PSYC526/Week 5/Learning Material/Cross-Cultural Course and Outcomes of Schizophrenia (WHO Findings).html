Cross-Cultural Course and Outcomes of Schizophrenia (WHO Findings)

One of the most intriguing and debated findings in cultural psychiatry comes from the World Health Organization (WHO) International Study of Schizophrenia and related longitudinal research [website].

Early WHO International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (IPSOS) and International Follow-up Study (IFS) data from these large, multi-national cohorts, initially indicated a better long-term course of schizophrenia (i.e., defined by symptom remission and social functioning) among individuals in “developing” countries compared to industrialized areas. This has been referred to as the “outcomes paradox” and suggested the potentially protective roles of close social networks, extended family support, and valued social roles in certain societies that may mitigate against the high rates of chronic disability in industrialized settings.

Later follow-up and more critical consideration of study attrition and methodological issues have pointed to a more complex picture, with many caveats and some more nuanced evidence that differences in outcome may be partially accounted for by treatment availability, access to care, stigma and social integration, both within and across countries. What remains clear, however, is the role of cultural and social factors (expectations, support, and roles) in shaping the course of this illness.
