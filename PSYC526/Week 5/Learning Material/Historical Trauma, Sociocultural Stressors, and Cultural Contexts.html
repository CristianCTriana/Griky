Psychotic Disorders & Severe Mental Illness

Historical Trauma, Sociocultural Stressors, and Cultural Contexts

Historical trauma is a way of thinking about the collective psychological, social, and health consequences of large-scale, devastating experiences, such as colonization, forced migration, genocide, and cultural repression (Sevillano et al., 2022; Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018; see also Week 3). Historical trauma has been conceptualized and studied more in the context of depression, suicidality, and substance use, but there is emerging literature that considers how chronic structural violence and intergenerational stress may influence pathways into psychosis. The proposed mechanisms include stress proliferation, inflammatory and neuroendocrine dysregulation, social defeat, and disruptions to cultural identity and collective meaning-making systems.

For Example

In studies with Indigenous peoples, Holocaust survivors, and forcibly displaced groups, researchers have found higher levels of risk for psychosis-related symptoms, including paranoid ideation and dissociative experiences, when historical trauma is paired with chronic socioeconomic adversity, marginalization, or structural discrimination (Sevillano et al., 2022).

Historical trauma frameworks push back against individualistic and pathologizing explanations of psychosis by contextualizing collective distress in political, historical, and racialized processes. They also pose ethical questions about the risk of pathologizing communities facing chronic collective stress and decontextualizing the structural factors that shape mental health. In cultural formulation, these frameworks can be used to think more broadly about how meaning-making, ancestral memory, and community narratives are implicated in experiences like hearing voices, having intrusive thoughts, or feeling altered states of consciousness without assuming a biomedical cause.

These intergenerational processes intersect with other socio-environmental stressors with well-established associations with psychosis risk. Urbanicity, migration, low ethnic density in the community, and low socioeconomic position are all epidemiologically established risk factors for schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders.

For Example

Immigrant populations have been shown to have a higher risk of psychosis, especially when they are in communities where they are in the ethnic minority. These associations have been described with several hypotheses about the impact of chronic social stressors, including social isolation, discrimination, social cohesion, and economic stress, which may interact with individual-level risk to trigger or exacerbate psychosis symptoms. These relationships are still being untangled, but the larger literature on the social determinants of serious mental illness also describes the links between discrimination and social adversity and the onset and severity of psychotic symptoms and long-term outcomes (Castillejos et al., 2018).

Both the historical trauma and socio-environmental stress research lend support for biopsychosocial models of psychosis that take social context, culture, and social structure into account along with neurobiological and psychological factors. This broader approach is also in line with the larger arguments in cultural psychiatry and global mental health scholarship, which make the case that psychotic disorders are not only a matter of individual illness but can also represent patterned, social, structural, and historical responses to chronic stress and systemic violence.
