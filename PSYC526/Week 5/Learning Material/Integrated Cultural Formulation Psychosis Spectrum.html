Week 5
Psychotic Disorders & Severe Mental Illness

Integrated Cultural Formulation: Psychosis Spectrum

Please see the table below for a summary of information from the week’s content.

Summary
Domain Psychosis Spectrum
Cultural Concepts of Distress Cultural metaphors for psychotic symptoms include: Spiritual discourse, ancestral communion, spiritual possession, moral failure or sin, disease or biomedical disorder.
Explanatory Models Different models overlap: Biomedical (dopamine dysregulation, neurocognition), social defeat/trauma, displacement, historical trauma, spiritual etiologies, religious causation, stress-vulnerability models, pluralistic hybrid models.
Meaning & Valence Voices: Benevolent/relational vs. intrusive/malevolent. Delusional themes imbued with cultural meaning (persecution vs. spiritual mission). Cultural factors influence if voices impart agency, power, morality, personhood, and malevolence.
Social Response & Stigma Ranges from respectful accommodation/divination/religious roles, to stigma, marginalization, discrimination, and forced/aversive biomedical care. Family/community reactions play a crucial role.
Help-Seeking Biomedical psychiatry/psychotherapy, clergy/spiritual leaders, traditional/indigenous healers, community elders, and plural/hybrid pathways are considered.
Structural/Historical Context Migration, displacement, acculturation, structural adversity, discrimination, historical trauma, and collective violence increase risk. Urbanicity and low ethnic density are associated with higher incidence.
Protective Factors Family support, cultural acceptance of altered states, lower stigma, and pluralistic integration of healing systems may lead to better outcomes. Structural conflict, isolation, and chronic stress predict worse courses.
Clinical Implications Culturally attuned assessment centers on meaning-making, explanatory models, stigma, pathways to care, and structural context rather than symptom counts alone.
