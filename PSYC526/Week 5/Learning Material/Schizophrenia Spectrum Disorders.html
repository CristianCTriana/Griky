Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders characterized by psychosis were called “schizophrenic spectrum disorders” in the past. Psychosis in this context broadly refers to hallucinations, delusions, thought disorder, and similar symptoms that signal a loss of contact with reality. In the current DSM-5-TR, we find a category of schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders. Schizophrenia has not changed much as a category except for the removal of subtypes (e.g., paranoid, disorganized). The spectrum now more strongly emphasizes symptom severity and course of the condition over a subtype. We will talk about specific schizophrenia spectrum disorders, but schizophrenia itself is clearly severe in terms of loss of functioning (APA, 2022).

In schizophrenia and schizophrenia spectrum disorders, people experience hallucinations and delusions. Delusions are fixed, false beliefs. Hallucinations are experiences that occur in the absence of external stimuli. We may also see disorganized speech or disorganized and catatonic behavior. In negative symptoms, we would see symptoms like diminished emotional expression and avolition, which is a lack of motivation. These psychotic symptoms must persist for a specified period of time to reach diagnostic criteria, and they are severe enough to cause significant distress and loss of functioning in the individual’s life.

There are clear genetic influences for risk of schizophrenia. However, this genetic influence does not account for the complete picture, so other factors are at play. Some theorists have focused on the interaction between genes and the environment. Many people in this condition have been found to have abnormalities in neurotransmitter systems, particularly dopamine. Researchers have focused on altered dopaminergic activity in this condition. These findings have informed neurobiological models of schizophrenia and the dopamine hypothesis. In addition to biology and environment, there is also a clear social and environmental risk factor in early adversity for developing schizophrenia.

A number of models have been proposed for understanding schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders, given the complex interaction of possible contributing factors that we just reviewed. There are neurobiological models, cognitive models, and models that attend to contextual, situational, and social-cultural factors that can interact with symptoms.

Schizotypal Disorder

DSM-5-TR: Schizotypal Personality Disorder is now in the chapter on Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders rather than in the chapter on personality disorders. This change is meant to reflect its phenomenological and (partially) etiological continuity with schizophrenia, while also clarifying that it is a separate condition that has a less severe course and degree of functional impairment (APA, 2022).

The essential feature of schizotypal personality disorder is “a pervasive pattern of social/interpersonal deficits and cognitive/perceptual distortions and eccentricities of behavior” (APA, 2022, p. 733). Symptoms of schizotypal personality disorder are pervasive and persistent. They begin during adolescence or early adulthood and may be stable or continue to become more severe over time, as with other personality disorders. Compared with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, this condition is less severe, and there is an absence of full psychosis or delusions/hallucinations. The DSM-5-TR describes the following symptoms: “ideas of reference (not delusions), odd beliefs and/or magical thinking that are inconsistent with cultural norms, unusual perceptual experiences, suspiciousness/paranoid ideation, constricted or inappropriate affect, odd speech (e.g., vague, circumstantial, metaphorical), eccentric appearance or behavior, and marked social anxiety that is often disproportionate to the actual threat posed by a social situation and not better explained by fear of negative evaluation (APA, 2022, p. 733). Importantly, this social anxiety is often driven more by paranoid fears of others than by a fear of evaluation. A substantial body of work supports the idea that high schizotypy (and schizotypal personality disorder) is associated with milder-but-detectable cognitive vulnerabilities, including working memory, attention, and executive functioning (all domains that are also impaired in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders).

In a recent meta-analytic synthesis of the schizotypy literature, Diao and colleagues (2023) report reliable weaknesses in working memory among individuals high in schizotypy, which they interpret as consistent with a neurocognitive vulnerability profile.

Schizotypal features often appear by adolescence and can be long-lasting, but the severity and impairment may vary (Lindstrom et al., 2025). Note that not all individuals who meet criteria for this disorder will go on to develop schizophrenia or any other psychotic disorder; however, some research suggests that risk for later non-affective psychosis is meaningfully elevated in some cohorts, especially in combination with other risk factors like substance use (Lindstrom et al., 2025). In a recent large longitudinal register-based study, non-trivial rates of transition from schizotypal disorder to non-affective psychosis were reported over long-term follow-up, and substance use disorders were a significant risk amplifier (Lindstrom et al., 2025).

There may be the potential for more targeted and tailored approaches to psychotherapy with this disorder type. In one pilot randomized controlled trial, researchers compared a novel psychotherapy that was designed for the treatment of STPD to a combined cognitive therapy/psychopharmacology condition (Cheli et al., 2023). The results provided preliminary support for the idea that more targeted psychotherapy can lead to clinically meaningful change for individuals with STPD. Cognitive and functional interventions may also be relevant: Cognitive remediation, social skills training, and medication have all been shown to improve cognitive and functional outcomes, relative to a behavioral platform alone (Lindstrom et al., 2025). In a recent systematic review, Gundersen and colleagues (in press) similarly conclude that the literature on pharmacologic augmentation and psychosocial interventions for cognitive and functional impairment in this disorder is emerging but highly heterogeneous, underscoring the need for stronger trials but supporting multimodal approaches that combine cognitive and social-functional rehabilitation.

When we are evaluating the symptoms of “odd beliefs,” perceptual experiences, and interpersonal style, cultural context is key. Some beliefs (e.g., spiritual practices, culturally sanctioned magical or religious interpretations) may be normative in a given cultural group. We should not be pathologizing beliefs that are normative within a given cultural system unless those beliefs are actually idiosyncratic within that community. Even then, we have to ask whether they are causing distress or functional impairment to the individual (APA, 2022). In practice, this will mean that culturally responsive assessment includes questions about the patient’s cultural identity, beliefs that are shared with others in their community, their meaning systems and beliefs about the sources of their experiences, and their idioms of distress.

Brief Psychotic Disorder

A Brief Psychotic Disorder is a schizophrenia spectrum disorder that is marked by the sudden onset of at least one of the following psychotic symptoms: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, or grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior. This change in functioning lasts at least one day, but no more than one month, and there is a subsequent return to premorbid functioning (APA, 2022).

This category is used when the person has psychotic symptoms, as described above, but the course and the outcome are different than the other psychotic conditions that we have seen. So, even if the symptoms might be severe and very concerning, the person has what is called a time-limited illness course. This contrasts schizophrenia or the schizophreniform type, where we are seeing symptoms that have to last for a certain period of time, and we know that there will be a longer-term impact. A Brief Psychotic Disorder is differentiated from schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, and mood disorders with psychotic features by its acute onset, brief duration, and favorable short-term prognosis. Clinically, brief psychotic disorder often presents with an abrupt disruption of reality testing, which often occurs in the context of marked psychosocial stress or emotional turmoil. The DSM-5-TR also recognizes specifiers that further characterize the presentation, including onset with marked stressors (brief reactive psychosis), without marked stressors, and with postpartum onset, the latter typically emerging within four weeks following childbirth (APA, 2022). Disorganized speech and behavior are particularly common, and affective instability may be prominent, though mood symptoms do not meet criteria for a mood episode that would better explain the psychosis. Empirical research supports the association between acute stress exposure and brief psychotic episodes, particularly in reactive forms of the disorder. Systematic reviews indicate that individuals diagnosed with brief psychotic disorder show elevated rates of recent stressors and trauma, compared to those with chronic psychotic disorders, supporting stress-vulnerability models rather than progressive neurodevelopmental pathology (Fusar-Poli et al., 2016). Neurocognitive impairment, when present, is typically mild and transient, distinguishing BPD from schizophrenia-spectrum disorders with enduring cognitive deficits.

With a brief psychotic disorder, an individual experiences symptoms for a short time and can then return to premorbid functioning. However, we have to be cautious about assuming that this outcome is benign for every case. Longitudinal research is somewhat mixed on this, but there is evidence that individuals who meet criteria for brief psychotic disorder are at risk for later schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or mood disorder with psychotic features. This appears to be particularly likely if the symptoms are not in response to an obvious stressor, or if the person had evidence of poor functioning before the onset of the condition. Meta-analytic and longitudinal cohort data indicate that diagnostic stability for brief psychotic disorder is limited, with conversion rates to schizophrenia-spectrum disorders ranging from approximately 20–50% over extended follow-up periods (Fusar-Poli et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2021). Predictors of poorer prognosis include younger age at onset, insidious rather than abrupt symptom emergence, absence of clear precipitating stressors, family history of psychosis, and persistent negative symptoms following symptom resolution. These findings underscore the importance of careful longitudinal monitoring, even after apparent remission.

Schizophrenia

We have already reviewed schizophrenia’s key features under the last video on the schizophrenia spectrum. As we will see in Week 4, schizophrenia remains a challenging condition to clearly delineate, although both the DSM and ICD have made an effort to focus on the core symptoms of schizophrenia (delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder, negative symptoms). The ICD-11 has updated its approach by de-emphasizing subtypes, which has been a part of the DSM approach in previous editions and is still included in the DSM-5-TR.

Schizophrenia is a disorder with disturbances in the experience of perception, cognition, emotion, speech, and behavior. We understand schizophrenia as a heterogeneous disorder, which means that individuals who meet criteria for this disorder can vary in their symptoms and their course. It is a disorder that can have a long-term impact on the individual and that often requires lifelong management. In line with a dimensional view of this disorder, current nosological approaches (DSM and ICD) have moved away from subtyping. Research suggests that symptoms of schizophrenia vary over time and can fluctuate. This long-term instability makes categorical subtypes both unreliable and uninformative for clinical care.

In addition to neurobiological models, we also see a focus on neurodevelopmental and neurocognitive disruptions in schizophrenia. Cognitive impairment has become more salient and more viewed as a core feature of schizophrenia rather than a downstream consequence of psychosis. Meta-analytic data in this area have found reliable and consistent deficits in working memory, processing speed, executive functioning, and social cognition that predict poorer social and occupational functioning (Vita et al., 2024). Neurobiological models, in turn, point to the possibility of alterations in dopamine signaling—especially within mesolimbic and mesocortical pathways, in addition to glutamatergic and GABAergic dysregulation. Neuroimaging and genetic studies provide evidence for the complex polygenic neurodevelopmental condition and that it is also shaped by interactions between biology andenvironment. Environmental risk factors we see are obstetric complications, urbanicity, cannabis exposure, and socioeconomic adversity.

There is increasing evidence that negative symptoms and cognitive deficits are now recognized as more predictive of long-term functional outcomes than positive psychotic symptoms. This is supported by research that shows that negative symptoms such as diminished motivation and lack of emotional expressivity show limited treatment response to antipsychotic medications and has led to a focus on psychosocial and cognitive-behavioral interventions that can target functional impairment (Lindstrom et al., 2025). Illness trajectories are also much more heterogeneous than we might assume from the descriptions of schizophrenia that we often see: longitudinal cohort studies have found that schizophrenia is not always progressive or deteriorating. Over time, there are often periods of stabilization, and many people with schizophrenia show improvement or even partial recovery when given sustained psychosocial support and community-based care (O’Connell et al., 2018; Smeland et al., 2020).

Cross-cultural research on schizophrenia has also shown that there are both universal features and important variations in symptom interpretation, course, and outcomes. There is now a longer history of cross-cultural research, and research conducted through the WHO in the 1970s and 1980s has shown that there are different outcomes associated with this disorder, and individuals in lower- and middle-income countries often experience better functional outcomes than in high-income countries—at least in earlier decades. These results have been partially attributed to stronger familial involvement, cultural interpretations of psychosis that emphasize relational or spiritual meaning, and differences in social expectations about recovery. More recent critiques stress that these resource disparities must be contextualized within changing treatment landscapes and that methodological and reporting changes in these datasets are also important to consider, but the broader point that culture shapes course and prognosis in important ways has remained a point of consensus (Lindstrom et al., 2025).

In addition to differences in social support and family involvement, cultural meaning-making around hallucinations and delusions can be an example of how the symptoms of schizophrenia intersect with broader cultural concepts of the mind, self, and what is real. For instance, ethnographic work comparing hallucinations in three countries (the U.S., Ghana, and India) found that people in non-Western contexts were more likely to describe voices as playful, relational, or as having a special or spiritual significance, rather than as malevolent or pathological (Ng et al., 2025). This is an interesting and salient finding that suggests that symptom valence (how positive or negative a symptom is for the individual) may be shaped by culturally shared models of personhood and agency. This is an example of the theme we learned in Week 1 and Week 2 that psychiatric classification names one level of explanation for complex experiences that are also always embedded in cultural worlds.

Schizoaffective Disorder

Schizoaffective disorder is defined by the DSM-5 as a persistent psychotic illness in which an individual experiences both psychotic symptoms (e.g., hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thought) and a major mood episode (either depressive or manic) for the majority of the illness course, with at least two weeks of psychosis in the absence of mood symptoms; this diagnostic distinction differentiates schizoaffective disorder from pure mood disorders with psychotic features, where psychosis occurs exclusively during mood episodes (APA, 2022). This condition appears to be less common than schizophrenia and has a higher incidence in adults 25–35 years of age. Lifetime prevalence has been estimated to be 0.3% for schizoaffective disorder.

Etiological models are again multifactorial, incorporating familial genetic risk factors associated with both schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, neurobiological abnormalities (e.g., white matter, hippocampal), and environmental stressors.

Although definitions have been refined in the DSM-5 to improve diagnostic reliability, this condition has been controversial and has sparked ongoing debate about the validity of this category as distinct from a point on a psychosis-mood continuum. The ICD-11 definition is similar to the DSM-5, but also specifically states that these mood episodes are not better accounted for by a mood disorder. Clinically, we often see a combination of antipsychotic medications as well as mood stabilizers or antidepressants, depending on the symptom presentation.
