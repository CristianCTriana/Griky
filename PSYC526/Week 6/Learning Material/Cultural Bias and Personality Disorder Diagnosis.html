Cultural Bias and Personality Disorder Diagnosis

Empirical evidence supporting this stance comes from several converging areas of research. Epidemiological research has established that there are racial, ethnic, gendered, and national differences in estimates of the prevalence of PDs. However, what is still unclear is whether these differences reflect actual population-level variation in prevalence, culturally influenced differences in the expression of symptoms, or are instead artifacts of the diagnostic or measurement process itself. For instance, avoidant or dependent features may be more likely to be perceived as pathological in individualistic cultural contexts that prioritize assertiveness and independence, while narcissistic or antisocial features may be more socially reinforced and less likely to provoke concern. These cross-cultural differences raise the possibility that PD diagnoses are in some way contingent on culturally normative expectations about selfhood, emotional experience, and interpersonal functioning, rather than denoting universal forms of dysfunction (Fiorillo et al., 2024; Monaghan & Bizumic, 2023).

Clinical vignette, record-review, and structured assessment studies have highlighted how clinicians’ cultural assumptions and implicit biases can influence diagnostic decision-making; some research suggests that certain racialized groups, especially Black men in the United States, may be more likely to be assigned antisocial personality disorder labels or externalizing diagnoses, while women’s personality pathology is more likely to be conceptualized in terms of borderline constructs (Krueger & Blaney, 2023; Moleiro, 2018). These issues intersect with larger discussions about gender, power, and structural inequity in psychiatric nosology and raise the question of whether PD criteria may unwittingly pathologize marginalized identities or culturally sanctioned coping styles. Measurement bias may also underlie diagnostic disparities, as PD assessment tools have been primarily developed and normed in Western samples, with limited cultural validation in non-Western or minority cultural groups (Monaghan & Bizumic, 2023).

Scholars in cultural psychiatry have suggested that PD diagnosis is particularly susceptible to bias because so many of the central elements of diagnosis (e.g., interpersonal style, emotional expression, personality traits) are all influenced by culture (Yildiz et al., 2025).

Norms about how to express anger, dependency, self-presentation, relational obligations, and how to avoid interpersonal conflict can all shape how symptoms are manifested and subsequently evaluated. For example, behaviors that align with borderline PD in one cultural context (e.g., intense affective expression, fears of abandonment) may be expressed differently or mitigated by collectivistic family structures in another. By contrast, some traits associated with narcissism or antagonism may be more ideologically or socially rewarded in individualistic competitive contexts. These factors can make it difficult to determine whether an individual is distressed or impaired and raise the need to disentangle culturally congruent behaviors from personality pathology.

Cross-national research has also compared mean trait levels and trait-behavior relations across cultures, and it has found that cultural variables (e.g., individualism–collectivism, social orientation, cognitive style) are in fact associated with how people express those traits and which traits are adaptive (Oltmanns & Widiger, 2020).

For Example

People may interpret certain behaviors as confident or assertive in one culture, and antagonistic in another, while traits that have been associated with interpersonal warmth may be more protective in collectivist cultures.

Clinical Implications

Recent work has emphasized contextualized trait assessment (assessments that tap into the ways that a trait is expressed within a cultural norm) and the use of role-based analyses that link traits to social outcomes in each culture (rather than assume a single global standard of what is and is not adaptive). These findings suggest that clinicians should interpret personality self-report measures and diagnostic thresholds relative to cultural norms and expected social roles.
