Personality Disorders & Cultural Norm

Cultural, Structural, and Sociohistorical Factors

How do culture and context influence the expression, interpretation, and pathologization of personality traits?

Examples from cross-cultural psychology and cultural psychiatry can illustrate how different cultures have different cultural scripts regarding the normative expression of emotion, interpersonal relatedness, concepts of selfhood, and autonomy (Fiorillo et al., 2024). Traits that might superficially resemble dependent or avoidant PD may be normative or adaptive in collectivistic cultures that prioritize interdependence, deference, and relatedness, while self-enhancement and assertiveness may be more socially sanctioned or rewarded in individualistic cultural contexts. Affective volatility or relational instability associated with borderline PD may be expressed differently or have different functions in cultures with different norms for affective expression, family cohesion, or conflict avoidance. We can see how assessment frameworks attempt to disentangle culturally congruent behaviors and normative distress from clinically impairing pathology that is inconsistent with a person’s cultural group or context using methods of cultural formulation and cultural- or dimensional-informed assessment (APA, 2022; Hopwood et al., 2019).

Structural and sociohistorical contexts also have a strong influence on the development of our personalities and interpersonal styles. Discrimination, migration, socioeconomic disadvantages, and other forms of structural inequality or collective trauma can result in a defensive interpersonal style (hypervigilance, mistrust, guardedness) that is easily misattributed to personality pathology in the absence of a contextualized assessment (Fiorillo et al., 2024). Scholars in cultural psychiatry have long been critical of the tendency to medicalize survival strategies that were a result of oppressive structural contexts, not the personality system itself. Dimensional approaches like the AMPD and ICD-11 models may be less culturally biased, especially if they emphasize severity and level of impairment in functioning rather than culturally loaded categorical traits (Oltmanns & Widiger, 2020).

For Example

The AMPD distinguishes (A) impairments in personality functioning (self and interpersonal aspects) and (B) pathological personality traits (five broad domains: Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Antagonism, Disinhibition, Psychoticism, further divided into specific facets).

Research in the past decade has consistently shown strong convergence of AMPD Criterion B traits with other well-established five-factor models of personality, and the trait model has the advantage of improving reliability and dimensional measurement of severity. There are still debates about the added value of the functioning criterion (Criterion A), with some research challenging the incremental validity of this assessment over and above the trait model, and there are also well-known issues in implementation in clinical practice settings. It can be helpful to teach this model alongside the categorical DSM criteria so that students can appreciate how contemporary research and clinical practice are trending in a more pragmatic direction that is also more aligned with global mental health, cultural humility, and patient-centered care.

Finally, we can discuss treatment research that debunks the common misconception that PDs are static or untreatable. Evidence-based treatments (DBT, MBT, Schema Therapy, TFP, etc.) have accumulated an extensive literature showing that we can meaningfully improve symptom severity and functioning, especially for borderline PD, using different therapeutic models (Cheli et al., 2023; Volkert et al., 2019). Longitudinal research also highlights the variability in PD course, with many people achieving remission or functional recovery, which is also moving the field away from fixed categorical views of PD toward developmental and dimensional formulations of psychopathology (Monaghan & Bizumic, 2023). This debate reflects larger tensions in our field between descriptive nosology, empirical models of personality, and cultural norms around selfhood, emotion, and interpersonal behavior.

For graduate students, PDs provide an excellent case study in how psychiatric classification systems change considering scientific evidence, sociocultural trends, and innovation in treatment research.
