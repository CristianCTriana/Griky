Personality Disorders & Cultural Norm

Developmental & Etiological Models of Personality Disorders

Personality disorders can develop from highly polygenic and multifactorial developmental pathways. Specific dimensions of temperament, attachment, neurocognition, and environment throughout childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood are thought to contribute to the formation of personality disorder. In longitudinal studies of temperament, high negative emotionality and disinhibition in early childhood has been predictive of later personality psychopathology in a diathesis–stress model of biological predisposition and contextual adversity. In developmental psychopathology, research has consistently pointed to a categorical–dimensional model in which PDs are maladaptive variants of normative personality, integrated into the dimensional model of DSM-5 AMPD and ICD-11 (Krueger et al., 2023).

Attachment theory has had a substantial influence on theories of personality pathology. Disorganized attachment and other forms of insecure attachment have been identified with borderline personality disorder and other cluster B presentations by virtue of emotion regulation, mentalization, and interpersonal trust processes. Mentalizing and social cognitive models have been proposed to underlie interpersonal instability in BPD and mistrust/detachment in cluster A presentations (Gagliardini et al, 2022).

In all cases, attachment trauma and loss are conceptualized as developing a vulnerability, rather than a deterministic causal relationship.

PDs are also informed by neurobiological and neurocognitive findings of impairments in neural systems that mediate emotional and reward reactivity, impulse control, and social cognition. Borderline personality disorder, for example, has been associated with amygdala hyperreactivity, limbic–prefrontal disconnection, and increased stress reactivity, while studies of antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) and psychopathy have identified aberrations in fear conditioning and moral decision-making, as well as diminished empathic awareness. Overall, this research supports the conceptualization of PDs as dysregulations across affective, cognitive, and interpersonal systems rather than stylistic behavioral differences (Gagliardini et al., 2022; Monaghan & Bizumic, 2023).

Environments also plays a key role in forming developmental risk. Trauma, emotional invalidation, neglect, and chronic stress exposure are frequently studied environmental experiences that have a significant impact on the development of PDs. Studies of emotional invalidation and developmental trauma in borderline personality disorder, for example, have shown that interpersonal contexts that punish or dismiss emotional expression can adversely affect emotion regulation development, compounding temperamental vulnerabilities. Overall, PD developmental models tend toward gene–environment interaction rather than fixed trait models.
