Personality Disorders & Cultural Norm

Global Mental Health & PDs

Research on global mental health provokes inquiry into whether constructs of PDs map onto culturally universal dimensions of personality pathology, or culturally specific models of self, moral character, and social functioning. Personality attributes, interpersonal expectations, and norms around autonomy and dependence, emotional expression and regulation, conflict and hierarchy, and other facets of personality show marked cultural diversity. Such cultural variation challenges the view that PDs reflect biologically universal syndromes that cut across cultures as opposed to culturally relative patterns of deviation from a normative model of functioning.

Comparisons of mean levels of personality traits across countries provide evidence of both cross-culturally universal trait structures (such as Big Five/Five-Factor Model dimensions) and large cultural differences in the expression, evaluation, and perceived adaptiveness of particular traits and trait profiles.

Theorists have used these cultural contrasts to question whether PDs present in Western nosologies are culturally specific products of norms and expectations around self and relationships or represent actual psychopathology (Fiorillo et al., 2024).

For Instance: Social avoidance or deference might be a well-adapted or at least culturally appropriate interpersonal style in a culture organized around collectivism and interdependence, filial obligation, and hierarchical relationships, while assertiveness, competitiveness, and self-enhancement are rewarded in an individualistic culture.

ICD-11 reformulated its PD model to be more globally applicable by prioritizing severity and degree of functional impairment rather than relying on specific trait definitions that are anchored in Western models of personality. This change would be consistent with the dimensional model of personality structure but also likely motivated by cultural psychiatry perspectives that caution that diagnosis of personality pathology must take into account sociocultural standards and expectations, valued community roles, and normative interpersonal relationships (Monaghan & Bizumic, 2023; Oltmanns & Widiger, 2020). A central critique of Western models of PDs is that they might over-pathologize culturally normative or even valued expressions of dependency, emotionality, and relational obligation, while failing to recognize pathological variants of narcissism, antagonism, or other traits that are modeled and reinforced by cultural ideals.

Global Mental Health Reflection

Universality versus cultural specificity of personality psychopathology is still an open empirical question, but the global mental health movement provides useful prompts to students and clinicians to consider how PDs present in different cultural contexts and normative groupings, how baselines of normality and abnormality are defined, and whether distress or impairment is evidence of pathology or a mismatch with cultural or structural conditions.
