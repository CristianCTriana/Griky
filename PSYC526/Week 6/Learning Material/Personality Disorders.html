Personality Disorders & Cultural Norm

Personality Disorders

Personality disorders (PDs) are defined as a “pervasive pattern of inner experience and behavior that deviates markedly from the expectations of the individual’s culture” (APA, 2022, p. 645). This deviance is expected to be present across multiple domains, such as cognition, affectivity, interpersonal relatedness, and impulse control, and lead to significant distress or impairment by adolescence or early adulthood (APA, 2022). The DSM-5-TR endorses the categorical model and the three clusters based on descriptive features: Cluster A (odd/eccentric), Cluster B (dramatic/erratic), and Cluster C (anxious/fearful). Although this classification has served as a useful heuristic, its empirical validity and prognostic utility have been challenged repeatedly, as clusters are based on surface-level similarity rather than on etiology, mechanisms, or treatment response (Krueger & Blaney, 2023).

Recent models have increasingly moved towards dimensional and trait-based approaches that view PDs as maladaptive variants of otherwise normative personality traits.

The DSM-5 AMPD and ICD-11 PD model both move away from categorical syndromes towards characterizing PDs as disorders of personality functioning (e.g., identity, self-direction, intimacy, empathy) and pathological personality trait domains (e.g., negative affectivity, antagonism, detachment, disinhibition, anankastia, psychoticism) (Hopwood et al., 2019; Oltmanns & Widiger, 2020). These models have been driven by scholarship in personality psychology, developmental psychopathology, and neuroscience, integrating classification with decades of empirical data showing that PDs are dimensional, comorbid, and heterogeneous rather than discrete and mutually exclusive categories (Monaghan & Bizumic, 2023). These models also align with longitudinal findings that show that PD features often change over time, and symptomatic remission is common (particularly for borderline personality disorder).

Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)

Key features of BPD include affective instability, impulsivity, identity disturbance, and intense, unstable relationships (APA, 2022). Research supports that BPD is strongly associated with trauma exposure, attachment disruptions, and affective reactivity. Treatment development has been a key area of research, with approaches like DBT, MBT, TFP, and schema therapy demonstrating improvements in symptoms and functioning. Longitudinal research has found higher rates of remission than previously thought, challenging the stereotype that BPD is chronic or untreatable (Volkert et al., 2019).

Antisocial Personality Disorder (ASPD) & Psychopathy

ASPD is characterized by disregard for the rights of others, impulsivity, irresponsibility, and violation of rules (APA, 2022). Psychopathy (not isomorphic with ASPD) emphasizes interpersonal-affective features, including callousness, manipulativeness, and shallow affect. Neurocognitive approaches implicate impairments in fear conditioning, empathy, and reward processing. Research on psychopathy is often conducted in forensic settings, but community-based presentations are more heterogeneous and less violent. Gender differences are substantial, with ASPD more frequently diagnosed in men. This raises questions about how behavioral norms are gendered in diagnosis.

Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD)

NPD is characterized by grandiosity, a need for admiration, and lack of empathy (APA, 2022). Contemporary research has highlighted a distinction between grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, the latter being linked to shame, hypersensitivity, and dysregulation. Cultural, gender, and socioeconomic factors strongly influence when narcissistic traits are pathologized versus rewarded, which may connect NPD research to sociocultural debates about competitiveness and self-enhancement.

Avoidant and Obsessive–Compulsive PDs (AVPD & OCPD)

AVPD and OCPD are some of the most prevalent disorders in clinical populations, with frequent comorbidity with anxiety and mood disorders. AVPD includes hypersensitivity to criticism, avoidance of social interactions, and fear of rejection, whereas OCPD features perfectionism, rigidity, and need for control. The dimensional model in ICD-11 connects both to elevated anankastia and negative affectivity traits, and AVPD may share developmental pathways with social anxiety disorder, which has raised some debate about classification.
