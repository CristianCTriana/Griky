Neurodevelopmental Psychopathology

ADHD: Dimensional, Lifespan, and Cultural Perspectives

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is diagnosed when there are age-inappropriate levels of inattention that interfere with academic, occupational, or relational functioning, as well as hyperactivity and impulsivity (APA, 2022). According to DSM-5-TR, ADHD is diagnosed when both symptom clusters and age of onset are met. Compared to DSM-5-TR, ICD-11 defines attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder as part of neurodevelopmental disorders and allows for more comorbidity (WHO, 2022). Specifically speaking to cultural changes and dimensional diagnoses, ICD-11 no longer allows for ADHD subtypes and instead specifies attention-deficit symptoms versus hyperactivity/impulsivity symptoms.

Neurocognitive & Neurobiological Models

ADHD is moving toward being defined by impairments to neural networks (e.g., connectivity and communication), executive function, and reward pathways (Poddar et al., 2025). Studies have shown that cortical development (i.e., particularly prefrontal areas related to working memory) are delayed. Computational psychiatry models add on this by quantifying within-person variability in reaction times and prolonged distracted behavior.

Developmental Considerations & Comorbidity

Symptoms of ADHD can change throughout development. Hyperactive symptoms tend to decrease with age, but ADHD also co-occurs with problems with emotion regulation and attention problems into adulthood. ADHD symptoms often become occupational issues, debt, and relationship problems in adults. There is high comorbidity with anxiety, depressive disorders, autism spectrum disorder, specific learning disorders, ADHD, Conduct Disorder, aggression, depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, and physical symptoms (Petruzzelli et al., 2025). Symptoms of emotional dysregulation are now included in the diagnosis of ADHD and may impact treatment as well as level of impairment. ADHD also meets criteria for impairments in several RDoC domains.

Gender, Masking, and Underdiagnosis

Boys and girls can be affected differently by ADHD. Boys are more likely to be seen for ADHD because of their hyperactivity levels, but girls can develop ADHD with inattention, anxiety, or an internalizing presentation (Gershon & Gershon, 2022).  Girls and women with ADHD symptoms tend to compensate for their inattention or disorganization until they reach college or workforce settings and are given more freedom to move around. Many women come to treatment for ADHD only after seeking care for anxiety, mood, or unexplained physical symptoms.

Cultural & Cross-National Variation

There are differences in ADHD diagnosis around the world. Countries may see higher rates of ADHD due to how children are expected to behave or in more structured school systems where children are expected to sit still for long periods. Studies show that ADHD is less likely to be diagnosed in Black children and Hispanic children, and when they are diagnosed, they are less likely to be put on stimulants compared to their White peers at similar symptom levels (Schonwald et al., 2025). Factors include structural barriers, clinician implicit bias, school system differences, and medical mistrust.

Neurodiversity Perspective

The biggest critique of the medical model of ADHD is from the lens of neurodiversity. ADHD symptoms are explained by a difference in brain physiology and function, rather than variations in cognition. Some strengths that individuals with ADHD may have are creativity, out-of-the-box thinking, and can hyperfocus on subjects of interest. Context matters when looking at how ADHD can cause impairments; if you are in a stimulating environment that allows for fast task switching, you may be less impaired than in a traditional classroom (Poddar et al., 2025).
