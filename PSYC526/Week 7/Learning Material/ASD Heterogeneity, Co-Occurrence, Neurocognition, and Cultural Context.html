Neurodevelopmental Psychopathology

ASD: Heterogeneity, Co-Occurrence, Neurocognition, and Cultural Context

DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 define Autism Spectrum Disorder by enduring deficits in social communication and interaction across multiple contexts; restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or sensory responsiveness (APA, 2022; WHO, 2022). ASD is heterogeneous across language proficiency, cognitive profile, adaptive functioning, sensory processing patterns, and social motivation. Both manuals have adopted a spectrum-based approach. Compared with the DSM-5-TR, ICD-11 includes increased specification about functional impairment, and allows for co-diagnosis of Autism and ADHD. This change was made in recognition of research documenting high levels of phenotypic overlap and shared genetic risk factors between autism and ADHD (APA, 2022; WHO, 2022).

Neurocognitive and Developmental Mechanisms

Studies using various lenses from developmental neuroscience have identified disruptions to systems involved in theory of mind, social reward processing, and joint attention. Sensory sensitivities/restrictions (hyper/hypo, sensory seeking, sensory overload) are central to ASD symptomatology and relate to emotion regulation and participation in activities (Tafolla et al., 2025). Motor symptoms were considered less central in previous diagnostic criteria, but delayed motor milestones and poor coordination may also be early signs of autism. Findings from genetic risk modeling converge on a highly polygenic etiology that involves synaptic organization, neurodevelopmental timing, and transcriptional regulation; prenatal risk factors such as maternal flu/infection and low birth weight also confer a small but significant increase in risk (Tafolla et al., 2025). Further, longitudinal studies investigating the developmental cascade of ASD show the condition emerges across multiple levels of biology, environment, learning, and social experiences, rather than through a specific pathway.

Co-Occurrence and Other Points of Consideration

ADHD, anxiety, intellectual developmental disorder, specific learning disorder, and language disorder all commonly co-occur with ASD (Petruzzelli et al., 2025). Clinicians will note that previous editions of the DSM did not allow for dual diagnosis of Autism and ADHD; both DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 now allow these diagnoses to be made concurrently (APA, 2022; WHO, 2022). This change reflects empirical evidence that deficits in attention and executive functioning are present in many individuals with ASD and ADHD presentations can alter the developmental trajectory of ASD. These are just two examples of how our nosology is changing to reflect that separating constructs into rigid categories may be artificial and ignore underlying neurodevelopmental mechanisms. Frameworks such as RDoC allow for symptom overlap across constructs like attention, social cognition, and sensory processing.

Masking Behaviors

Masking behaviors can also play a role in diagnosis or lack thereof. Masking refers to camouflaging one’s natural way of social communicating by consciously or unconsciously mimicking neurotypical social behaviors (e.g. practicing conversations ahead of time, forcing eye contact). Often this requires significant cognitive or sensory processing for autistic adolescents and adults. Studies suggest that masking is more prevalent in girls and women with Autism and contributes to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis in adulthood, as well as risk for internalizing symptoms and burnout. Masking can also have cultural features: expectations for reciprocity, eye contact, affective expressivity, and conversation are nuanced across cultures. Autistic features can be perceived and responded to quite differently based on cultural contexts and disability awareness (Bradley et al., 2021; Chapman, 2020).

Cultural & Global Context

ASD presentation and diagnosis are deeply influenced by cultural norms and resources. International studies demonstrate wide variation in prevalence, age of diagnosis, and access to services. Contributing factors include structural inequalities like availability of trained clinicians, school services, and cost of evaluation; as well as cultural attitudes about neurodevelopmental differences, mental health, and disability (Pham & Charles, 2023). Additionally, there are cultural differences in how autistic traits are perceived and expressed. In some cultures, sensory symptoms or restrictive interests may be more likely to be seen as personality/today differences. Global mental health research suggests that Autism is underrecognized among females, racial/ethnic minority groups, and in low resource settings where behaviors may be better accommodated by family and community members (Napolitano et al., 2022). As a result, members of these groups are less likely to receive diagnoses when seeking care and experience intersecting challenges related to stigma, medical mistrust, and teacher/school expectations.

Culture, Context, Neurodiversity

Much of the history of the neurodiversity movement is grounded in autistic self-advocacy. Neurodiversity challenges traditional “deficit” models of Autism that focus on correcting and normalizing social behaviors (Kapp, 2020). Under this framework, autism is considered a variation in social attention, sensory processing, learning, and perception. Neurodivergent becomes pathological or causes distress largely due to ableist environments and social norms. Thus, neurodiversity connects concepts from disability studies (e.g. access, accommodations, rights) with cultural psychiatry (i.e. meaning in context, role expectations, stigma). In psychopathology classrooms, we can use neurodiversity as a lens to question whether suffering is due to pathology within the person, a sociocultural mismatch, or lack of access—while at the heart of debates on classification and global mental health.
