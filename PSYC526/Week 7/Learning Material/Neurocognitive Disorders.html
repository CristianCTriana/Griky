Week 7
Neurodevelopmental Psychopathology

Topic name 

Neurocognitive disorders (NCD) are defined by acquired deterioration in cognition compared to prior ability across one or more domains including learning/memory, complex attention, language, perceptual motor, or social cognition (Livingston et al., 2020). Mild neurocognitive disorder (MCI), per DSM-5 criteria, represents a subtle clinical presentation where the cognitive deficits do not interfere with instrumental activities of daily living, while major NCD involves obvious cognitive symptoms that interfere with independence (APA, 2022). ICD-11 criteria conceptualize neurocognitive disorders similarly but places more emphasis on functional impairment and subtyping by etiology (WHO, 2022).

Etiology and Variants

Major NCD should be thought of as a category of disorders with different underlying causes rather than one distinct disease (Livingston et al., 2020). Causes of NCD include Alzheimer’s disease, vascular disease, Lewy body disease, Parkinson’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, traumatic brain injury, HIV infection, and toxic/metabolic causes. Alzheimer’s is currently the leading global cause of NCD and is associated with episodic memory decline early in the disease course, followed by atrophy in the temporal and parietal lobes and build-up of the proteins amyloid-β and tau. Vascular disease caused by stroke or cumulative risk tends to have more prominent executive and processing-speed deficits. Lewy body and Parkinsonian disorders share a few key features of diurnal fluctuation in attention, visual hallucinations, REM sleep behavior disorder, and parkinsonism. As you can see, there are shared symptoms across different causes but established patterns of cognitive, neurological, and functional features that can help differentiate these disorders.

Differential Diagnosis

MCI and NCD can often be difficult to differentiate from depression, schizophrenia spectrum illness, or neurodevelopmental differences. Pseudodementia refers to the presentation of depression that often includes perceived cognitive deficits that improve with treatment of mood. Some psychiatric conditions, including schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, can impact social cognition or executive functioning without underlying neurodegenerative disease (Emmady et al., 2022). Intellectual disability and ADHD are both examples of conditions where there would be no expected increase from baseline in cognition. Make sure to compare to prior levels of functioning and education when making a clinical diagnosis. Delirium is an important condition to consider in the differential, as it can present with confusion and attentional deficits. However, it often has an acute onset with a reversible cause like infection, metabolic disturbance, and medication change.

Cultural Considerations

Culture, primary language, and educational access play a large role in how neurocognitive disorders are presented and assessed. Many neurocognitive tests are heavily reliant on language, reading ability, and semantic knowledge that may be specific to western countries and/or higher education levels. Testing often uses established norms based on highly educated samples from the U.S. and Europe which do not translate well to global populations. For example, someone who did not receive much formal education or speaks multiple languages may be incorrectly diagnosed with NCD due to low testing familiarity. A study conducted in China found major disparities in dementia screening across racial groups and individuals of lower socioeconomic status, with suggested causes including lack of access to screening, care giver beliefs about symptoms, and not meeting expected impairment (Nielsen, 2022). Due to strong familial relationships in eastern countries, family can often help mediate the functional decline experienced by someone with major NCD, changing the perceived level of impairment used in Western definitions of the disorder.

Dementia rates are expected to increase in low- and middle-income countries while they continue to stabilize or decrease in many high-income countries. These countries will not see the same increase in diagnosed cases due to lack of access to screening and resources. Additionally, these countries do not have as large of support systems for family caregivers or widespread access to nursing home care. The WHO is working to address dementia as a global health problem by developing more culturally appropriate resources for interventions, caregivers, and community-care vs just expanding biomarker testing (Emmady et al., 2022).
