Neurodevelopmental Psychopathology

Neurodiversity and the Sociocultural Context

The Week 7 neurodevelopmental disorders have been at the center of sociopolitical discourse and academic activism related to neurodiversity, disability justice, and psychiatric nosological limits. The autistic community and advocacy groups were foundational in conceptualizing neurodiversity frameworks, which have expanded to include ADHD, learning disabilities, dyspraxia, dyscalculia, and other neurodevelopmental conditions (Kapp, 2020). Neurodiversity discourses position autism and other neurodevelopmental conditions as natural variations in human behavior that influence how people think, feel, sense, and navigate their environment.

Overlap of Neurodiversity

Cultural norms surrounding attention, reciprocity, communication, sensory perception, learning style, etc. influence what is considered adaptive versus impairing. (Chapman, 2020; Kaiser Weaver, 2019). For example:

  • Aspects of ASD that might be considered impinging on social communication (e.g., avoiding eye contact, pedantic language, rigid routines) can be normative or expected behaviors in communities that value respect for elders, social harmony, or consistency
  • Behaviors associated with ADHD (hyperactivity, sensation seeking, loose associations) might not be impairing in contexts that prioritize improvisation and adaptability over long-term sitting attention.

Cross-cultural psychologists have found that school systems, family expectations, and work accommodations play a major role in determining “impairment.” Similarly, global mental health scientists have found that countries vary in how many individuals are diagnosed with ADHD or ASD, due to education systems, awareness, stigma, and health-system capacity more than “true” prevalence rates.


Neurodiversity and Social Justice

Neurodevelopmental diagnosis can intersect with other systems that impact their access to needed resources (insurance, academic support, healthcare services). Systemic factors that disproportionately affect certain groups include lack of:

  • Access to healthcare for diagnoses
  • Appropriate assessments/screeners
  • Economic and educational supports at home
  • School-based accommodations
  • Public insurance that covers treatments

In the U.S., we see that:

  • Children from racialized minority groups are diagnosed with ADHD/ASD less often than their white counterparts despite reporting similar symptomology.
  • Girls, multilingual children, Black and Latinx children, and children from low-income families are diagnosed with ASD later than their counterparts.
  • We see similar disparities in neurodevelopmental diagnoses in adults.

Thus, it is important to look beyond biological explanations for the prevalence of neurodevelopmental disorders. Our ability to understand how institutions, policy, and larger systems affect the care our patients receive is known as structural competency.
