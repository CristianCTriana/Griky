Somatic Symptom & Related Disorders

The category of somatic symptom and related disorders (SSRDs) refers to a group of disorders characterized by psychological responses to somatic sensations, perceived illness, or concerns about functioning that are clinically significant but NOT by any particular underlying medical pathology. In DSM-5-TR, mental health professionals moved away from previous categories of somatoform disorders by eliminating the criterion that symptoms must be “medically unexplained.” Instead, there is an increased emphasis on maladaptive cognitive-affective–behavioral responses to illness (e.g., catastrophic thinking, health anxiety, reassurance-seeking behaviors, avoidance, or excessive distress) (APA, 2022). SSRDs are now conceptualized more broadly within a biopsychosocial framework where an individual’s distress, coping behaviors, and impairment are highlighted.

Cultural Idioms of Distress and Somatization

Across cultures, people tend to describe their emotional distress with some combination of somatic and psychological terms. In some cultures, it may be considered inappropriate or taboo to openly discuss emotional difficulties. In these situations, somatic symptoms may be the preferred or socially accepted way to express one’s psychological pain. The tendency to express distress through somatic symptoms has been labeled an idiom of distress (Kaiser & Weaver, 2019). When someone tells you they are experiencing a “somatic idiom of distress,” they are sharing their suffering, perceived threat, or problematic relationship through bodily symptoms.

Below are a few examples of somatic idioms of distress from around the world:

Susto

Typically seen in Latin America; sickness or illness that occurs when something causes the soul to leave the body. Symptoms may include tiredness, trouble eating, nausea, and social withdrawal.

Kufungisisa (“thinking too much”)

Zimbabwean Idiom of distress that links distress to somatic weakness.

Shenjing shuairuo (Chinese for “nerve weakness”)

Historic disorder seen in China that includes symptoms of somatic weakness, mental fatigue, and distress.

Dhat

South Asian idiom of distress that involves semen loss and accompanying feelings of weakness and anxiety.

Hwa-byung

A Korean idiom of distress characterized by anger that has been suppressed or unexpressed. Symptoms include feelings of bodily tension, chest pain, and heat sensations in the body.

The symptoms noted above illustrate that the manner in which individuals describe psychological distress varies greatly. In fostering cultural responsiveness, it is essential to remember that when we operate from a Western mental health lens, we may be biased toward “psychological” symptoms (e.g., feelings of sadness, irritability).

Help-Seeking, Stigma, and Differential Pathways to Care

Where do individuals go when they are struggling with somatic symptoms? Individuals might seek care from traditional healers, religious leaders, general medical providers, herbalists, friends, and family. This is just some of the many routes that exist when someone is seeking care for their mental health. Think about how these different pathways impact when a person might receive a diagnosis or start treatment, as well as duration of untreated distress.

In a clinical context, it is important to understand that some individuals from cultures different from your own may express their psychological distress through somatic symptoms. There is risk in assuming someone’s somatic symptoms are an SSRD and not caused by a legitimate medical condition. However, there is also risk in not considering SSRDs as a possible diagnosis. As always, it is important to consider the whole person and withhold judgment until gathering more information.

Beyond how individuals choose to discuss their symptoms, sociocultural factors play a role in someone’s willingness to seek care (Ntumi et al., 2025). For example, somatic symptoms may be less stigmatizing than psychiatric symptoms in some cultures. In other contexts, being diagnosed with a medical condition may impact someone’s ability to fulfill family roles, get married, or find acceptance within their community. Considering these cultural factors can help providers approach patient concerns without bias and can improve collaborative meaning-making.

Sociocultural Factors and Somatic Symptoms

When thinking about somatic symptoms, it can be helpful to also think about structural factors at play. Factors like inequality, difficulty accessing healthcare, job-related stress, and physical labor can influence how someone experiences and expresses their distress (Kaiser & Weaver, 2025; Ntumi et al., 2025). Research in global mental health has found that SSRDs are prevalent in refugees, migrants, and crisis-affected communities. Instead of indicating a specific medical disease, somatic symptoms (pain, fatigue, difficulty working) can be a manifestation of complex trauma, stress, and uncertainty about the future.
