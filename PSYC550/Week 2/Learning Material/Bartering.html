Week 2 Relationships and Boundaries

Bartering

When a client is unable to afford services, he or she may offer a bartering arrangement. Bartering is the exchange of goods or services instead of a monetary fee. Although this type of arrangement can allow for clients to obtain services, it presents a similar dilemma that we saw in the previous sections on boundaries and dual relationships. Considerations for bartering arrangements are applicable in all settings, including therapy, research, consulting, etc.

Is it appropriate for a therapist’s client to clean the therapist’s house, perform secretarial services, or do other personal work for the therapist? Clients can easily be put in a bind when they are able to learn personal information about a therapist. Depending on the perceived fairness of the barter, clients may feel they have been taken advantage of by the therapist, thus damaging the therapeutic relationship (Corey et al., 2020).

Evaluating Bartering Arrangements

Prior to entering a bartering relationship, Corey et al. (2020) suggest the clinician evaluate the following: whether it puts you at risk of impaired professional judgment, collaboratively determine the value of goods or services, determine appropriate length of time for arrangement, document the arrangement, and consult with experienced colleagues or supervisors.

Bartering is one way to work in collaboration with clients, using sound judgment, which considers the client’s culture. Bartering can be a dignified and honorable form of payment for those who are cash poor, but talented in other ways and is a healthy norm in many cultures.

Some suggest that bartering arrangements can be culturally sensitive and prove satisfactory to both parties. However, clinicians should fully assess the arrangements of the barter before entering the barter. It is recommended that one seeks consultation from trusted colleagues who can provide an objective evaluation of the proposed arrangement.

Practitioners need to ensure that they are not imposing valued that come from a cultural perspective different from their clients. Thomas (2002) maintains that bartering should not be ruled out simply because of the slight chance that client might initiate a lawsuit against the therapist.

Venturing into multiple relationships requires careful thought and judgment, and in making decision about bartering, the most important issue is the welfare of the client. Furthermore, written contracts that details the bartering arrangement should be reviewed regularly.

Accepting goods or services for professional service can lead to the slippery slope phenomenon, which could result in perceived or actual exploitation, boundary violations, or reduced effectiveness as a clinician. Barnett and Johnson (2008) suggest it is unwise to engage in bartering with therapy clients.

The practitioner should ensure:


The bartering arrangement is in the best interest of your client;
It is reasonable, equitable, and undertaken without undue influence;
It does not get in the way of providing quality psychological services.

Corey et al. (2020) offer the following guidelines to clarify barter arrangements: minimize unique financial arrangements and exchange goods rather than services. Both therapist and client should have a written agreement for the compensation by bartering. If a misunderstanding develops, the matter should be dealt with by a mediator, not by you and your client.

If one does enter into a bartering agreement, Campbell et al. (2010) recommend documentation of the following:

  1. Rationale for bartering
  2. Discussion with the client about the advantages and disadvantages
  3. Consultation with other psychologists about the matter
  4. Specific bartering conditions agreed upon by the practitioner and the client

Gifts in Therapy

Very few of the professional codes of ethics specifically address the topic of giving or receiving gifts in detail. Lavish gifts present an ethical dilemma; however, refusing a small gift from a client may damage the therapeutic relationship, especially if is part of the client’s culture. It is recommended that practitioners cover gifts in their informed consent process that is discussed with clients at the onset of the relationship.

When a gift is no longer purely a symbol of gratitude, even a small gift with an ulterior motive or manipulation, raises ethical concerns. Timing is another important issue with gifts. It may be more appropriate for the gift to occur at the end of the relationship rather than the beginning.

A survey of active professionals revealed that most practitioners are willing to accept a gift if:

Inexpensive Culturally appropriate Sign of appreciation at end of treatment

Ultimately it is the practitioner’s responsibility to determine the meaning of the gift and if it is appropriate. There are differing opinions on social relationships with clients. Some offer that practitioners and clients are able to handle such relationships as long as the priorities are clear. Others contend that counseling and friendship should never be mixed.
