Week 2 Relationships and Boundaries

Boundary Management

A common type of boundary crossing is practitioner self-disclosure. This applies to all settings in which the professional practices, including therapy, research, consulting, forensics, etc.

In therapy, this occurs when a practitioner self-discloses personal information. Before sharing personal information, practitioners must consider the client’s history, presenting problems, client’s comfort with the practitioner disclosures, and the practitioner’s comfort with disclosing. Again, it is important to consider the motives for sharing the personal experience.

Barnett and colleagues (2007) indicate that if a practitioner’s actions result in any harm to a client, it is considered a boundary violation. Failing to adhere to ethical standards can result in well-intentioned action being perceived as a boundary violation. Often, behaviors done at the wrong time or in the wrong situation can derail the professional relationship and cause harm to the client.

There may be situations in which boundary crossing is appropriate. For example, it may be culturally consistent for boundary crossing to occur and refusing to engage in boundary crossing may be a lost opportunity to build rapport and develop the relationship.

It is recommended to find a balance for the professional relationship that is neither too tight nor too loose. A flexible boundary can help clients develop trust in the professional relationship. There are also advantages of out-of-office experiences which may be beneficial to some clients. Examples include:

Home visits Attending celebrations of a client Outdoor therapy Other appropriate encounters with clients

For example, accompanying a client to the gravesite of a loved one for whom the client has not grieved.

Practitioners are encouraged to consult with their insurance carrier about such practices as these activities may have implications for one’s liability exposure.

Culture & Solidarity

Culture is another important factor in determining boundary crossing. Speight (2012) proposes the concept of solidarity, which is a culturally congruent way of understanding, defining, and managing boundaries.

Adopting this concept, a practitioner will be able to be genuine in professional relationships without being inappropriate or exploitative. In essence, one maintains flexible boundaries that are culturally consistent with the client, even though it may be inconsistent with other boundary viewpoints. Again, be mindful of the line between boundaries that are too close and those that are too distant.
