Week 2 Relationships and Boundaries

Considerations for Sexual Relationships

Pope et al. (1986) addressed the resounding lack of systematic research on sexual attraction of therapists to their clients. Their findings among active practitioners reveal a complex reality:

82%

Reported they had never seriously considered sexual involvement with a client.

94%

Report they had never engaged in sexual relationships with clients.

90%

Thought it was unethical to reveal a sexual attraction to a client (2011).

Interestingly, most reported the fear of negative consequences and the need to uphold ethical values, while on a minority of the respondents indicated values pertaining to the client’s welfare as a reason not to engage in sexual relations.

Experiencing sexual attraction to a client (and without acting on it) makes the majority of therapists feel anxious, guilty, and confused. It is therefore not surprising that most therapists hide these feelings rather than acknowledge and discuss them with colleague or to their own therapy.

There is a distinction between finding a client sexually attractive and being preoccupied with this attraction. If you find yourself in this situation, it is important to monitor these feelings and examine this issue in your own therapy.

Practitioners who have difficulty setting boundaries with their clients will be more likely to encounter ethical dilemmas. It is important for therapists to recognize countertransference reactions and deal with them in an effective manner to ensure these feelings do not get buried.

Strategies to minimize the likelihood of sexual transgressions (Corey et al., 2020):

  1. Recognize attractions and deal with them constructively.
  2. Seek support during loss or crisis.
  3. Continually monitor feelings and behaviors.
  4. Know the difference between attraction and action.
  5. Learn possible adverse consequences.
  6. Maintain clear boundaries against advances.
  7. Terminate if objectivity is obscured.
  8. Recognize risks of explicit disclosures.
  9. Acknowledge caring and warmth instead of sexual feelings.
  10. Practice a risk management approach.
  11. Use supervision and personal therapy.

Unfortunately, many clients do not know that sexual contact between a practitioner and client is unethical and illegal. Sexual relations with a client are one of the most damaging boundary violations in the profession. All major ethic codes strictly prohibit it.

Impact of Misconduct

Investigations continue to show that clients who are the victim of sexual misconduct by a professional suffer dire consequences. Harm to the client may include mistrust of the opposite sex, hospitalization, and even suicide. Other potential effects include emotional, social, and sexual intimacy issues.

Criminal Liability & Professional Sanctions

The two major causes of criminal liability for therapists are having sex with clients (and former clients) and fraudulent billing. In many states, it is a criminal offense.

Therapists can face:

Lawsuits Felony conviction License revocation Expulsion from organizations Loss of insurance

Clients can file civil suits, criminal actions, or ethical complaints with the state’s licensing board. The board has the power to discipline the therapist, which may include probation, mandatory therapy, and close supervision if allowed to practice again.
