Week 2 Relationships and Boundaries

Considerations for Social Relationships

Corey et al. (2020) raise concerns about social relationships with clients for the following reasons:

Reduced Challenge

Practitioners may not be as challenging as they need to be with clients because they do not want to damage the friendship.

Loss of Objectivity

Practitioners’ own needs may be entangled with those of the client and will result in the loss of objectivity.

Risk of Exploitation

Practitioners are at greater risk of exploiting because of the even larger power differences created in the relationship.

Western ethics and standards strongly discourage dual and multiple relationships, claiming that such relationships can create a hindrance to the client’s well-being. With some cultures, a social relationship may facilitate a greater connection with the practitioner (Corey et al., 2020).

Practitioners are not legally or ethically prohibited from engaging in nonsexual relationships with clients who have terminated treatment; however, this may be problematic.

Example: A former client and practitioner become friends after treatment is terminated. A year later, the practitioner receives a request to present testimony on the client’s mental status in the preceding year for a law suit.

State Licensing Boards & Bans

Some state licensing boards view social relationships with former clients the same as sexual relationships with former clients. In some states, there is a social and sexual relationship ban with former clients for two or more years after the termination of therapy. This includes dating, getting married, or becoming friends.

Despite the appeal of creating a social relationship with previous clients, those former clients may need you more as a practitioner than a friend in the future.

Practitioners should be cognizant of their and their former client’s motivations. As a profession, we ought to question those who use their professional services to build a social network (Corey et al., 2020).
