Ethical Analysis

Boundary crossing or changes in role are a departure from commonly accepted practices that could potentially benefit the client. Boundary violations or exploitation of the client are a serious breach in the relationship that results in harm to clients and is therefore unethical.

Not all boundary crossings are boundary violations. However, boundary crossings can lead to the blurring of professional roles and the relationship with the client.

Practitioners can apply an ethically based decision-making model to facilitate decision-making. Younggren and Gottlieb (2004) recommend that practitioners address these questions to made valid decisions about multiple relationships:

  • Is entering into a relationship in addition to the professional one necessary, or should I avoid it?
  • Can multiple relationships potentially cause harm to the client?
  • If harm seems unlikely, would the additional relationship prove beneficial?
  • Is there a risk that multiple relationships could disrupt the therapeutic relationship?
  • Can I evaluate this matter objectively?

When answering these questions, the practitioner needs to assess the risk for conflicts of interest, implications for the professional relationship, and a loss of practitioner objectivity. Practitioners must discuss the potential problems of engaging in multiple relationships with the client and involve the client in the decision-making process. Even after assessing the relationships and deciding it will be of benefit to the professional relationship, the practitioner should use the greatest of caution when entering the relationship.

A few other guidelines to increase the likelihood the client’s best interests are being served:

  • The practitioner is motivated by what the client needs rather than by his or her own needs.
  • The client’s history, culture, values, and diagnosis have been considered.
  • The rationale for boundary crossing is documented.
  • The boundary crossing is discussed with the client in advance to prevent misunderstanding.
  • Full recognition is given to the power differential, and the client’s trust is safeguarded.
  • Consultation with colleagues guides the practitioner’s decisions.
  • In therapy settings, the boundary crossing (explained below) is consistent with a client’s treatment plan.

There are some behaviors for professionals that are not considered to be multiple relationships. For example, accepting an invitation to a client’s graduation, bartering goods or services for professional services, accepting small gifts, attending the same social, cultural, or religious activities, or giving a client a supportive hug after a difficult session.
