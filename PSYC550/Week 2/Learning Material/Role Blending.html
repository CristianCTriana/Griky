Week 2 Relationships and Boundaries

Role Blending

Role blending is the combining of roles and responsibilities. For example, school practitioners must often function as practitioner, teacher, and chaperone. Supervisors typically engage in many different roles: coach, consultant, mentor, evaluator, and practitioner.

Role blending is not necessarily unethical, but practitioners need to ensure that exploitation does not occur. The occurrence of role blending in education is unavoidable, and it can present ethical dilemmas when there is a loss of objectivity or conflict of interests.

The Slippery Slope Phenomenon

Whenever potential negative outcomes exist, professionals have a responsibility to implement safeguards to reduce potential harm (Corey et al., 2020). Practitioners often get into trouble when boundaries are poorly defined. The slippery slope phenomenon or the small steps of loosening boundaries can progressively end up in harmful and exploitative behaviors (Gabbard, 1996).

With the existence of this phenomenon, therapists end up practicing in an overly cautious manner that may harm clients. Many believe that the slippery slope does exist, but it is not as steep or slippery as many fear. Most boundary crossings are done in a thoughtful manner and in appropriate situations. See Herlihy and Corey (2015) for a set of key themes surrounding role blending.

Flexible vs. Rigid Boundaries

Many professionals agree that flexible boundaries can be helpful when applied within the ethical guidelines and standards. Arnold Lazarus (1994) takes the position that certain boundaries and ethics diminish therapeutic effectiveness. He believes that some well-intended guidelines can backfire and that, rather than being driven by rules, it is best for therapists to engage in a process of negotiation in multiple relationships, whereas some dual or multiple relationships can in fact enhance treatment outcomes.

Critics of this stance suggest that some practitioners have difficulty determining where appropriate boundary lines should be drawn. A main concern with Lazarus’s stance on boundaries is that it may lead inexperienced therapists to misinterpret what is acceptable behavior and devalue the importance of respecting boundaries in therapy. Others claim that the goal of ethical decision making is to take the position where exploitation of the client is minimized.

It is very easy for a therapist to misuse power, and we should question the clinical purpose and impact of playing tennis, eating meals, or going for walks with clients. Boundary violations imbalance the already imbalanced therapist-client relationship, resulting in the needs of the more powerful person in control. Still others are concerned that Lazarus is dangerously close to not clearly defining the boundaries. Failing to establish clear boundaries can be dangerous for both the client and the therapist.

Barnett and Johnson (2010) suggest that rigid adherence to boundaries may be just as harmful to the therapeutic relationship as a boundary violation. For example, refusing a small gift from a client or refusing to extend a session for any reason. This rigidity is the result of the clinician lacking the ability to distinguish between positive boundary crossing and boundary violations.

Challenges in Small Communities

Managing boundaries are everyday situations faced by practitioners who live in small communities. In small (including rural) communities, mental health providers have greater challenges in dealing with multiple relationships than those working in urban areas. Some of these ethical dilemmas are unique to rural or small community practices.

For example, if a therapist shops for a new tractor and the only salesperson in town happens to be a client, the therapist risks violating the ethics code. However, if the therapist were to buy a tractor in another town, it may damage the relationship with the client.

Furthermore, some smaller communities operate mainly on swaps and barter rather than cash. This does not automatically create problems, but potential conflicts exist in the therapeutic relationship if bartering does not work out well. To gain credibility, practitioners must become an integral part of the community.

When making decisions about multiple relationships, it is essential to consider the risk of exploitation, loss of therapist objectivity, and harm to the professional relationship (Corey et al., 2020). To minimize risk in small communities, Corey et al. (2020) suggest practitioners should:

  • Maintain thorough dwocumentation
  • Obtain informed consent
  • Attend to matters of confidentiality
  • Establish clear boundaries and expectations
  • Participate in ongoing consultation
