Week 2 Informed Consent

Informed Consent and Therapy

In therapy, informed consent is a collaborative effort between the practitioner and client. It is critical that the client is competent to make the voluntary decision to enter treatment. Informed consent is obtained through verbal discussion and written confirmation.

The informed consent document generally includes the following components:

Initial assessment Treatment process Client rights/responsibilities Practitioner’s background Cost of treatment Length of treatment Termination Confidentiality Treatment interruptions

Additionally, the informed consent should include a description of the benefits and risks of participating in treatment, along with treatment alternatives. The practitioner must explain the initial assessment process, which may last a few sessions and focuses on gaining an in-depth understanding of the client.

The initial assessment involves extensive inquiry into many aspects of the client’s life:

Family Education Work Substance abuse Medical Mental health Religion/faith Immigration status Quality of life

It is reasonable for clients to be anxious in the beginning phase of treatment and as treatment approaches termination. They may also experience anxiety when they are making changes in their lives (Fisher, 2023).

It is important for the client to understand that there are no guarantees that treatment will have the desired outcome. If a practitioner uses a non-traditional treatment, it is important to communicate the advantages and disadvantages.

Alternative resources to traditional therapy:

Self-help groups Twelve-step programs Crisis services Support groups

The client has a right to know about the practitioner’s education and training, along with the credentials, licenses, and certifications. The practitioner should clarify if treatment is provided under supervision or by an intern.

Clients should understand the cost of treatment and payment methods, cost of missed appointments and last-minute cancellations, insurance coverage, and limitations to reimbursement.

In crisis situations, it may not be feasible to obtain informed consent prior to engaging in a therapeutic relationship. When the crisis is resolved, the practitioner should establish informed consent.
