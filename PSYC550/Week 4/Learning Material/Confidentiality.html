Week 4 Confidentiality, Mandated Preporting and HIPAA

Topic name

Confidentiality is a term used by psychologists that refers to the information that a client discloses to the practitioner. The APA Ethics Code (2017) requires all psychologists to take reasonable precautions to protect confidential information through or stored in any medium while recognizing that there may be laws, regulations, or organizational policy that impact these protections.

An important distinction to note is that confidentiality is a psychological term, whereas privileged communication and privacy are legal terms. Privileged communication generally bars the disclosure of confidential communications in a legal process. The privilege may vary in states, so it is important for the practitioner to understand the laws governing privilege in the state they are working. Privacy is the constitutional right of individuals to control their personal information.

Practitioners should attempt to protect confidentiality in all therapy settings; however, it is important for clients to know that legally-privileged communication does not apply to group therapy, couples therapy or family therapy. It also does not apply to therapy with children or adolescents. Practitioners should take care in protecting a client’s privacy. In many cases, privacy is violated unintentionally. For example, if a practitioner is teaching a class or writing a book, it is important to maintain the client’s privacy, changing the name of the client and altering any information that may identify the client. Duffy (2010) and Sink (2010) recommend the use of fictional cases.

Confidentiality is important to the therapeutic process. Practitioners have a legal and ethical obligation to protect what the client shares in these sessions. However, confidentiality is not absolute or guaranteed. It is important for clients to understand the circumstances in which information disclosed during sessions may be shared.

Fisher (2008) Six-Step Model

First, practitioners must be knowledgeable of the limits of confidentiality and the surrounding legal challenges. Statements in the informed consent should reflect the parameters of confidentiality in a clear manner. Practitioners should discuss confidentiality with clients, taking note of the client concerns and the response to clients.

Second, clients should be advised of the limits on confidentiality and agree to these limits before starting therapy.

Third, practitioners should obtain informed consent before any disclosures are made. Even though informed consent is obtained prior to treatment, it is best practice to obtain it from the client again.

Fisher (2008) suggest the practitioner should notify clients when legal requests for disclosure occur. The information provided should be limited to the extent it is feasible.

Fifth practitioners should avoid unnecessary breaches of confidentiality by maintaining policies designed to protect confidentiality, reviewing note-taking and record keeping practices (especially electronic storage), and encouraging clients to act on their own behalf.

Last, practitioners should discuss confidentiality with clients, colleagues, students, and legal professionals to foster greater awareness of confidentiality.

The limits of confidentiality should be documented in the informed consent and discussed with the client to ensure they have a clear understanding of the parameters (Corey et al., 2020). For example, clients should understand the information required by managed care providers. Once the information is provided to the managed care provider, the practitioner has no control over who sees it and how it is used. In some cases, clients prefer to pay for treatment on their own.

Additional limits to confidentiality include when office assistants handle confidential information, when a client discloses intention to commit a crime, and when court orders a practitioner to provide records. Confidentiality is limited when a client poses a danger to self or others, and in the case of suspected abuse or neglect of a child or vulnerable adult.
