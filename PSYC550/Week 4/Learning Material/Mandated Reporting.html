Mandated Reporting

Psychologists have a duty to warn and duty to protect. This requires a careful balance between client confidentiality and public safety. Practitioners should identify clients who may physically harm others, protect others from dangerous clients, and treat dangerous clients. Failing to protect the public is a liability for practitioners that may have civil damages. There are several circumstances when a practitioner may be at risk for civil damages, including when practitioners fail to diagnose or predict dangerousness, fail to commit dangerous individuals, and prematurely discharge dangerous clients from a hospital.

There are several cases that establish legal precedent for practitioner behavior:

The Tarasoff case establishes precedent for the duty to warn of harm to self or others and the duty to protect (Tarasoff v. Board of Regents of University of California [website]).

The Bradley Case (Bradley Center v. Wessner). is the precedent for the duty not to negligently release a dangerous client, and the Jablonski (Jablonski v. United States) case established the precedent for the duty to commit a dangerous individual.

In the Hedlund Case (Hedlund v. Superior Court [website]), the duty to warn was extended to anyone who might be near the intended victim and who also might be in danger.

The Jaffee Case (Jaffee v. Redmond [website]) established that the communication between licensed practitioners and their clients are privileged and protected from forced disclosure in cases arising under federal law (Corey et al., 2020).

When dealing with cases that involve the duty to warn and protect, practitioners should consider all appropriate steps to take and the potential consequences of them. It is important to obtain a prior medical and behavioral history. It is also important to be knowledgeable of the policy of your institution/organization. When faced with an immediate threat, do not hesitate to take steps to prevent harm to yourself. In these cases, practitioners must be careful to document all actions taken and the reasons for the course of action. You may need to make referrals in some situations.

Suicide Assessment

Practitioners are obligated to conduct a thorough suicide assessment of clients that includes risk factors, warning signs, and mitigating factors. When assessing suicide, practitioners should take direct verbal warnings seriously.

A history of suicide attempts, history of psychiatric treatment, depression, hopelessness/helplessness, alcohol/drug abuse, severe anxiety, and interpersonal stressors should be assessed.

It is also important for the practitioner to inquire about any recent suicides in the family or any significant changes to the client’s health, such as a serious or terminal illness. The practitioner should ask about the client’s support system, the absence of which raises the client’s risk. The practitioner should ask directly about the presence of a plan, intent, and means to follow through on the plan. The practitioner should also be aware of any client behaviors such as giving away possessions or revising wills (Corey et al., 2020).

Psychologists may disclose confidential information without consent as mandated by law in the following circumstances:

Provision of needed professional services Obtain professional consultation Protect the client or others from harm Obtain payment for services

(disclosure limited to the minimum amount of information necessary to achieve the purpose) (APA, 2017). It is the professional’s obligation to protect those who are unable to protect themselves. Psychologists are mandated by law to report information to legal authorities when there is suspected child, elder, or dependent adult abuse or neglect. Elder abuse may include physical abuse, sexual abuse, psychological/emotional abuse, neglect, abandonment, or financial/material exploitation. In some states, there are reporting requirements for domestic abuse or when a psychologist has foreknowledge that a crime will be committed. Additional information regarding these circumstances can be found in each state’s law and regulations governing the practice of psychology.

Please take a moment to review your state resources for mandated reporting which can be found at: State Training Resources for Mandatory Reporters of Child Abuse and Neglect - Child Welfare Information Gateway [website]

Duty to Protect & HIV Status

The duty to protect in cases regarding HIV status is not clearly defined by the law. In these situations, it is important to have an understanding of your state law and to seek consultation from individuals who have experience in this area. Breaking confidentiality because of HIV status is not considered a legal exception to confidentiality.

Practitioners should engage in direct communication with their clients about the danger of certain behaviors and risk to others. If a client refuses to use safe sex practices and refuses to notify partners, the practitioner must consider the most appropriate course of action. When disclosing information related to HIV, practitioners should follow the legal guidelines and protect the client’s privacy to the extent possible (Corey et al, 2020).
