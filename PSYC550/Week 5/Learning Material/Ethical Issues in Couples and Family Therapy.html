Week 5 Ethical Issues in Individual, Couples and Family Therapy

Ethical Issues in Couples and Family Therapy

Clinical work with couples and families is often based on systems theory. According to systems theory, interpersonal and psychological issues develop within the individual’s current environment and family. The individual’s symptoms are viewed as a manifestation of dysfunction in the system. Even when a family identifies a specific person as the problem, the issue is viewed within the context of the family's dynamics.

According to the American Association of Marriage and Family Therapists Code of Ethics (2015), there are eight primary areas of ethical responsibility including:

responsibility to clients
confidentiality
professional competence and integrity
responsibility to students, employees, and supervisees
responsibility to research participants
responsibility to the professions
fees
advertising

When individuals engage in family therapy, it is important for them to understand that the goals of therapy will be prioritizing the family needs over the individual.

Clinicians have a very complex responsibility- to balance the well-being of the individual and family. Fisher (2009) recommends that the clinician consider their ethical responsibilities to all members of the family versus a specific client. In family therapy settings, clinicians have an obligation to maintain confidentiality, with the exceptions being when mandated by law, to protect clients from harming self or others, when the clinician is a defendant in a civil, criminal, or disciplinary action related to therapy, and with a written waiver.

Clinicians also have a responsibility to take continuing education to stay current on developments in the field. A few graduate courses in family or couples therapy is not sufficient preparation to ensure competent practice in the field. Additional training and supervision are required. Clinicians have a responsibility to students and supervisees to avoid dual relationships. This can be challenging because many programs encourage students to explore their own family-of-origin issues and rely on interventions/processes that are like those used in treatment.

Clinicians have a responsibility to other professionals. For example, if a client is seeing another therapist for individual therapy, the family therapist should not proceed with family therapy without consulting the other clinician. Clinicians should not accept fees for referring clients to other therapists. At the beginning of treatment, clinicians should discuss counseling fees and payment, and whether there is cost related to cancellations and missed appointments. When advertising, clinicians should be honest in their level of competence, academic history, clinical training, and experience. The personal, educational, and experiential qualifications of the clinician have an impact on the treatment process. Bitter (2020) proposed several personal attributes that are essential in family therapists, including:

acceptance
engagement
care
confidence
courage
flexibility
openness to the diversity of family members

Family therapists should have insight into their own family of origin issues and how these may impact practice. For example, therapists should seek supervision especially in cases that may evoke an emotional reaction from the therapist, such as those involving infidelity, divorce, cultural biases, and emotional abuse. Supervision is critical to ensure that the client’s communication and concerns are not misinterpreted by the therapist.

Couples and family therapists should have graduate coursework in systems theory. Studying therapeutic approaches designed for the individual are not transferable to the couple or family. In addition to formal coursework, training for family therapists should include didactic coursework, clinical experience with families, and supervision by a competent family therapist (Goldenberg & Goldenberg, 2013). It is critical for therapists to have training with families of diverse cultures and ethnicities. Experiential methods, such as personal therapy and the exploration of family-of-origin issues, are also important to the professional development of the clinician. These methods give the supervisee the opportunity to develop insight into the personal issue that may impact transference and countertransference.
