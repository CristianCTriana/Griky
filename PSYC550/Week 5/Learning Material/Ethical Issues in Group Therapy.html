Week 5 Ethical Issues in Individual, Couples and Family Therapy

Ethical Issues in Group Therapy

Group therapy is a frequently used method of treatment. Clinicians should develop competencies in knowledge and skill in group therapy. To develop competency as a group therapist, clinical training should focus on knowledge and skills as they pertain to

task/work groups,
psychoeducational groups,
group counseling, and
group psychotherapy.

Participation in groups is an essential part of the training process for new clinicians, specifically in

training and supervisory groups,
self-exploration groups, and
personal therapy.

These groups present clinicians with an opportunity to experience the ethical challenges that are associated with group therapy. These experiences may not provide absolute answers for the ethical dilemmas that may surface in group therapy; however, they offer valuable opportunities for the clinician to gain exposure to them (Corey et al., 2020). It is important for the clinician to develop competency in diversity in group therapy.

Most professional organizations offer a guidance for clinicians working in group settings, including the Association for Specialists in Group Works (2012), the American Psychological Association (1993), and Hays et al. (2010). Clinicians should cultivate awareness of their own cultural identity, how culture and society have influenced their life experiences, and any personal biases and stereotypes, along with how these might impact group work.

Clinicians should also recognize that stereotypes and biases may emerge in the group process and should actively work to establish an egalitarianism. Clinicians should acquire the knowledge and skills to work effectively with diverse individuals.

Clinicians should assess each group member. The assessment should include a history of life experiences, along with an exploration of

cultural identity,
acculturation,
heritage,
sociopolitical background, and
experience of oppression.

Clinicians should be knowledgeable of the cultural norms of the group members and respect the roles of family and hierarchy. They should also be familiar with forms of healing that are valued in the individual’s culture, such as churches, temples, community centers, etc.

In group therapy, clinicians are likely to work with a co-leader. There are advantages and disadvantages to having co-leaders. Co-leaders who develop collaborative working relationships provide group members with an opportunity to observe how to balance responsibilities in a mutually supportive relationship. Complications to the co-leading relationship include

poor communication,
competition between the clinicians, and
overdependence on one clinician.

It is critical for group co-leads to identify obstacles to effective working relationships and seek resolution. Co-leads who do not resolve conflict effectively run the risk of splitting by group members. It is essential for clinicians to use supervision as an opportunity to explore personal views about co-leading groups. Informed consent is an important part of the group process. The informed consent should provide group members with information about the group process so that they can make an informed decision about whether they want to participate (Corey et al. 2020).

The informed consent should include information about the

type of group,
clinician credentials,
group interventions,
rights of group members, and the
benefits and risks of participating in treatment.

It should address cost and payment, appointment policies, insurance, confidentiality limitations, and alternatives to group therapy. Group therapy is not effective with every client. As such, it is critical to screen prospective group members for selection in a group. In the absence of screening, individuals may not benefit from the group process and experience discouragement with treatment (Yalom, 2005). Some clinicians do not screen group members due to time constraints or because it is impractical in their setting. Others object to the process based on theoretical beliefs. Corey et al. (2020) suggest that some level of screening is essential to preserve the integrity of the group and protect the other group members.

Motivation is an important determination of success in group therapy; however, even the most motivated individual may not be well-suited to group treatment.

Yalom (2005) recommends group therapy for individuals with issues related to relationships, confidence, generalized anxiety, and who lack meaning in life.

He opposes group therapy for individuals with traumatic brain injury, hypochondria, acute psychosis, paranoia, antisocial personality, or active drug/alcohol addiction.

Psychoeducational groups may not require screening, whereas psychotherapy groups should include some type of screening (Corey et al., 2020). Yalom (2005) proposes that clinicians prepare group members for the therapy by explaining the guidelines for group behavior, explaining the group process, establishing expectations for member participation, and identifying the overall goals of the group. These topics should be reviewed in the initial session.

Psychological Risks

Clinicians should discuss the psychological risks of participation in groups and ways to minimize these risks. Corey et al. (2014) identify several psychological risks that participants should know about. Members should be advised of the potential for disruption in their lives because of the group process. Members are encouraged to be completely open. Even though the clinician may emphasize the importance of keeping group discussion confidential, there is no assurance that members will respect this confidentiality. Confrontation may be used as long as it is not a harmful attack on another person.

Members should know that they have the right not to explore an issue. Scapegoating and coercion should not be permitted. Group contracts establish group expectations. When clinicians and group members are operating from the same expectations, the psychological risks to all participants are reduced.

Confidentiality is complicated in group situations. It is not recognized as privileged communication unless there is a legal exception. The clinician has an ethical obligation to protect confidentiality and inform the group members of the limits to confidentiality. While encouraged, there is no way to guarantee that group members will maintain confidentiality. Other limits to confidentiality include harm to self or others, child abuse, elder or dependent adult abuse, and in certain institutional settings such as prisons.
