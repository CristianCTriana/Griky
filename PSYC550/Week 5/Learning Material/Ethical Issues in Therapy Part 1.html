Week 5 Ethical Issues in Individual, Couples and Family Therapy

Ethical Issues in Therapy Part 1

A strong foundation in theory is essential to ethical clinical practice. A clinician’s theoretical position guides practice. Many clinicians develop a counseling style rooted in a specific type of theory (psychoanalytic, cognitive-behavioral, Rogerian, etc.) that reflects their personal interests, education, and clinical training. Some clinicians prefer an eclectic approach that integrates different theoretical concepts. It is important to remember that the theoretical approach and interventions must be appropriate to the client’s unique needs. Therapy is not a one-size-fits-all approach.

As a clinician, you must be able to recognize when your theoretical orientation is not suited to the client’s needs (Corey et al., 2020). Conducting therapy with a theoretical foundation is advantageous for the clinician and client. The theoretical framework serves as a tool to guide the therapy process. It provides the clinician with a way to understand and organize information about the client. It also serves as a tool for the therapist to communicate information to the client about his or her concerns, goals, and progress.

Many existing theories do not account for cultural diversity. When selecting an orientation, it is critical to understand the client in the context of culture and society. If the clinician’s theory does not include a cultural component, it is important for the clinician to supplement training to develop competency in this area.

It is important to remember that there is a division of responsibility in therapy. New clinicians are likely to assume too much responsibility for the client’s success. This can impact how they view themselves, and ultimately how they perform with clients. In the early sessions, it is important to establish a therapeutic contract that defines the division of responsibility.

The therapeutic contract is not the same as an informed consent. The purpose of the therapeutic contract is to establish the parameters of the therapeutic relationship regarding the client’s goals, interventions, and therapy outcomes. It does not have to be a written document; it may be a discussion that takes place over the course of several sessions. The clinician may take on more of the responsibility in the early phase of treatment; however, it is important for the client to be an active participant in treatment.

Clinicians who determine the content and course of every session are reducing their client’s accountability for treatment. Clinicians who work in managed care settings should explain how responsibilities are divided among the managed care provider, client, and clinician (Corey et al., 2020). At the onset of treatment, it is important for the clinician and client to identify treatment goals. Goals should reflect the client’s presenting problem. For example, if a client is presenting with depressive symptoms the goals should be targeted toward alleviating these symptoms. Establishing treatment goals is a collaborative effort between the therapist and client.

Goals should be objective, measurable, and specific. They should also reflect the client’s view of psychological health as defined by their respective culture.
