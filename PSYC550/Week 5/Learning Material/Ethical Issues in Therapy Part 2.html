Week 5 Ethical Issues in Individual, Couples and Family Therapy

Ethical Issues in Therapy Part 2

The context of therapy will impact the treatment goals.

In managed care, goals must be focused on the reduction of problematic symptoms and focused on psychoeducation of coping skills.

In crisis situations, the therapist may be more directive in identifying goals that are short-term and practical.

Goals in academic settings are likely to be therapeutic and educational.

With older adults in assisted living environments, treatment goals are likely to center on coping with relationships in the community and stress related to family and physical health.

Clinicians must be knowledgeable of the interventions being used, along with an awareness of the anticipated outcomes. It is important for the clinician to use techniques that are appropriate to the client’s presenting symptoms and diagnosis, and consistent with the client’s values.

Even though it is critical to use effective techniques, research emphasizes the therapeutic relationship as the most important dynamic in treatment outcomes (Lambert, 2011, 2013; Norcross, 2013).

Assessment and diagnosis are critical to therapy, as they guide the treatment process. Assessment is an ongoing evaluation of the client’s life situation. Assessment contributes to the identification of a diagnosis. A diagnosis is a specific disorder based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5) (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013). Diagnoses are dynamic, and subject to change because of the assessment process. As a clinician acquires more information about a client, the diagnosis may be modified.

Psychodiagnosis is a general reference to the client’s emotional and behavioral concerns. It may or may not be linked to the DSM-5. When a client presents with symptoms that are characteristic of more than one disorder, it is important for the therapist to differentiate between the two diagnoses. This is called differential diagnosis.

Psychological theories approach diagnosis differently. While not all theoretical orientations consider diagnosis important, diagnosis serves many important functions, specifically as a guide for the treatment process and as a requirement for insurance reimbursement.

Some psychoanalytic therapists value psychodiagnosis.

Adlerian oriented therapists place a great deal of importance on the assessment process as it relates to the client’s lifestyle and problematic patterns of relating, with less focus on diagnosis.

Existential therapists and Person-Centered therapists view diagnosis and assessment as detrimental to the therapeutic process. Both theories focus on the client’s internal beliefs and assumptions, and not on how these symptoms appear to the outside world.

Gestalt therapists value the client’s experience at the present time over diagnostic interpretations.

Behavior theorists perceive the assessment and diagnostic process as essential to treatment planning.

Cognitive-Behavioral therapists focus on the relationship between distorted thinking patterns and behaviors, with less interest in diagnoses.

Reality therapists focus on the client’s current behavior and what they want out of life. Traditional diagnosis is not valuable to them.

Feminist theorists disagree with the diagnostic system because it does not consider social factors related to dysfunctional behavior.

The postmodern approaches of solution-focused therapy and narrative therapy do not focus on diagnosis because they are concerned with a client’s strengths and competencies.

The assessment process is used in systemic therapies, such as family systems therapy, but diagnosis is not as important since the problems are seen in the context of the entire system or family.
