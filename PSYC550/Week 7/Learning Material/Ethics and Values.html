Week 7 Ethics in Multiculturalism, Diversity, and Spirituality/Religion

Ethics and Values

As helping professionals, it is imperative that we take an inventory of personal beliefs and values that might impact the counseling process. For example,

how do you feel about working with someone who has different values and beliefs?
Would you be able to remain neutral?
How would you know if your beliefs are influencing the counseling process?
Would you have any difficulty working with someone who viewed the world differently?
How would you work with clients who have different views about life and death?
Would you work with a client who had different values, or would you refer them to another professional?
Does working with a client require that you accept and approve of the client’s values? 

In the helping relationship, the professional is expected to work with clients from diverse perspectives and worldviews. It is critical for the professional to manage their personal beliefs and values to ensure that these do not impact the professional relationship. This process is called bracketing (Corey et al., 2020). 

While it may seem important for helping professionals to share personal values with clients, it is important to remember that the focus is the client. Even when the professional shares personal opinions or values with good intentions, the client may perceive the professional as being judgmental or lacking objectivity. 

Before sharing information, the professional must consider the motivation for sharing this information and the anticipated impact on the client. To uphold the ethical principle of justice, professionals must treat all clients equally, even those with differing values.  

A professional's values may have a significant impact on the professional relationship. As such, it is critical that professionals engage in value exploration. Value exploration is an opportunity to identify and clarify beliefs and values, as well as to consider how these may manifest in work with clients. 

Value imposition occurs when a helping professional attempts to sway a client to accept the professional's values or beliefs. Value imposition is a significant ethical issue. The professional should consider the motivation behind the value imposition. 

In therapy, conflicting values are not a sufficient reason to refer a client to another counselor. When value conflicts surface, the counselor should take the initiative to seek supervision and develop adaptive strategies to manage reactions to the conflict. It is the counselor’s responsibility to maintain objectivity. A referral to another provider should only be considered when the counselor lacks the skill to work with the client’s issues (Corey et al., 2020). While some counselors may see a referral based on value conflict as being in the client’s best interest, this type of referral may be discriminatory. 

Recently, there were two court cases filed against public universities by Christian students over the requirement that students cannot impose moral values on clients. In both cases, the judges ruled in favor of the universities because of the discriminatory nature of value imposition (Julea Ward v. Board of Regents of Eastern Michigan University; Jennifer Keeton v. Board of Regents of Augusta State University). To protect religious freedom, some states inserted “freedom of conscience” clauses in legislation. Even though these clauses are part of legislation, they oppose the spirit of the ethics codes.
