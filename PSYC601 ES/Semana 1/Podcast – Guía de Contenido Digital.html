Semana 1
Introducción a los Métodos Cualitativos

 

Welcome to PSYC601: Qualitative Methods. This is Week 1, and the topic is Introduction to Qualitative Methods. This week is your starting point for learning how qualitative researchers study meaning, experience, and context. If you have ever read a statistic and thought, "That tells me something, but it does not tell me what it feels like," you are already close to the logic of qualitative inquiry. Qualitative research helps you understand the human side of a phenomenon, including how people interpret what is happening and how social context shapes experience.

Let us begin with what you will learn this week. You will learn what qualitative research is and why it matters. You will learn how qualitative inquiry differs from quantitative research, not because one is better, but because they are designed to answer different questions. You will also begin learning two concepts that will stay with you throughout the course: the idea that the researcher is an instrument of inquiry, and the importance of reflexivity, which is the habit of reflecting on how your perspective influences the research process.

By the end of Week 1, you should be able to describe when qualitative methods are a good fit, what kinds of questions qualitative researchers ask, and what makes qualitative research systematic and credible.

If you are new to qualitative research, you might be carrying a misconception that qualitative work is informal, or that it is simply collecting stories without structure. This week is designed to replace that misconception with a more accurate picture. Qualitative research can be creative, but it is also rigorous. It requires planning, thoughtful data collection, and careful analysis.

So what is qualitative research, really? At its core, qualitative research is a systematic approach to understanding meaning and experience. It asks questions like: How do people interpret a life event? How do communities define what is normal or acceptable? How do people make decisions when they are uncertain? How do identities develop and change across contexts? These questions are not easily answered by measuring a variable on a scale. They require listening, observing, and interpreting in a structured way.

One way to understand qualitative research is to think about the type of knowledge it produces. Quantitative research often produces estimates and relationships: how much, how often, what predicts, what differs. Qualitative research often produces explanation and insight: how people describe something, what themes show up across experiences, how meaning is constructed, what tensions exist, and why a situation feels the way it does from the inside.

This does not mean qualitative research ignores structure. In fact, structure is what makes it credible. A qualitative researcher makes deliberate choices about participants, data sources, and methods. They decide what questions to ask and how to ask them. They record and document what they see and hear. They analyze data systematically, looking for patterns and differences, and they build interpretations that are grounded in evidence.

Let us make this concrete with an example. Imagine you are studying burnout among graduate students. A quantitative approach might measure stress levels, hours worked, and symptoms, then test what predicts burnout. That can be valuable. But what if you want to understand what burnout means to students? Some students may define burnout as exhaustion. Others may define it as loss of motivation. Others may define it as a moral failure, like they are not strong enough. Those meanings matter because they shape coping strategies and willingness to seek help.

Qualitative methods are especially useful when you are exploring a topic that is not well understood, or when you suspect that existing measures are not capturing the full story. They are also useful when you want to understand processes, such as how people move through a transition over time, or how a workplace culture shapes behavior. In these cases, the research goal is not to reduce complexity. It is to understand it.

Now let us talk about the researcher as an instrument. This is one of the most important ideas in qualitative research. In many quantitative designs, the goal is to minimize the influence of the researcher through standardized instruments and controlled procedures. In qualitative research, the researcher is part of the instrument. Your questions, your listening, your attention, and your interpretations shape the research.

That is why reflexivity matters. Reflexivity is not the same as bias. Reflexivity is the practice of noticing your assumptions and how they might influence what you see. For example, if you believe that burnout is mainly about workload, you may miss themes about belonging, uncertainty, or identity. If you assume that a participant's explanation is exaggerated, you may not probe in a way that reveals deeper meaning. Reflexivity helps you remain open and disciplined. It helps you avoid treating your first interpretation as the only interpretation.

Another key concept in qualitative research is credibility. Credibility is about trustworthiness. It is the idea that your findings should be supported by evidence and that your process should be transparent. Credibility comes from many practices: careful documentation, systematic analysis, and clarity about how you moved from raw data to interpretation.

You will learn more about specific credibility strategies later in the course, but even in Week 1 you can start building the mindset. Do not think of qualitative findings as opinions. Think of them as interpretations that must be supported, explained, and grounded.

Now, you might be wondering: how do qualitative researchers actually collect data? There are many methods, but some of the most common include interviews, observations, focus groups, and document or media analysis. The data might be transcripts, field notes, recordings, images, or written artifacts. The common theme is that the data is rich and contextual. It captures language, meaning, and interaction, not only numbers.

Finally, let us address a common question: can qualitative research be combined with quantitative research? Yes. Mixed methods research combines strengths by using qualitative inquiry to explore meaning and quantitative methods to test patterns, or by using qualitative work to interpret quantitative results. The choice depends on the research question and what kind of knowledge you need.

So as you move through Week 1, practice asking yourself: what kind of question am I asking? Am I trying to measure and compare, or am I trying to understand meaning and experience? Neither is wrong. The skill is matching method to purpose.

This week is not only about definitions. It is about a mindset shift. It is about learning to value context, language, and lived experience as legitimate data, and learning to treat interpretation as a disciplined research act.

Let us close with a few reflection questions to help you practice qualitative thinking.

First, think of a psychology topic you care about, such as stress, identity, belonging, grief, motivation, or resilience. What would a quantitative question look like for that topic? It might focus on prevalence, correlation, or predictors.

Now, what would a qualitative question look like? It might focus on how people describe the experience, what meanings they attach to it, or how context shapes it. Notice how the qualitative question opens space for interpretation.

Second, consider your own assumptions. If you were studying that topic, what would you expect to find? What would you assume participants mean when they use certain words? Reflexivity begins with noticing those expectations before you interpret the data.

Finally, ask yourself what credibility would require. If you made a claim about people's experiences, what evidence would you need to support it? How would you show your reasoning clearly?

These questions help you practice the habits that make qualitative research strong: matching methods to goals, reflecting on perspective, and grounding interpretation in evidence.
