Semana 2
Ética en la Investigación Cualitativa

 

Welcome back to PSYC601: Qualitative Methods. This is Week 2, and the topic is Ethics in Qualitative Research. In Week 1, you learned that qualitative research is a rigorous approach to understanding meaning, experience, and context. In Week 2, you focus on something that makes that rigor possible: ethical responsibility. Qualitative research often involves real people sharing real parts of their lives. That means ethical practice is not a checklist. It is a mindset and a set of decisions you make throughout the study.

When people hear "research ethics," they often imagine forms, approvals, and compliance procedures. Those are important, but they are not the whole story. In qualitative work, ethics shows up in everyday moments: how you invite someone to participate, what you say before an interview starts, how you respond when someone becomes emotional, and how you protect details that could identify a participant even when you remove names.

In Week 2, you will examine core ethical principles that guide research: respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. You will apply these principles to qualitative contexts where relationships are closer and boundaries can be more complex. You will also focus on informed consent as an ongoing process, confidentiality and privacy, power dynamics, and planning for sensitive topics.

By the end of the week, you should be able to anticipate common ethical risks and design practical protections that support both participant safety and research credibility.

Let us begin with the core principles, because they provide a framework for decision making.

Respect for persons is about autonomy. Participants have the right to decide whether to participate and what they share. Respect is not only about obtaining a signature. It is about ensuring people understand what they are agreeing to and that they feel free to say no.

Beneficence is about minimizing harm and maximizing potential benefit. In qualitative research, harm can look like emotional distress, loss of privacy, or social risk, such as workplace consequences if participation becomes known. Beneficence requires you to think ahead about risks and create practical controls.

Justice is about fairness. It asks whether the burdens and benefits of research are distributed fairly. Are you recruiting participants because it is easy to access them, even if participation creates risk for them? Are you excluding voices that should be included? Justice also matters when working with vulnerable populations.

Now, let us focus on consent, because this is where qualitative ethics often becomes more complex. Informed consent means participants understand the purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, and their rights, including the right to withdraw. But in qualitative research, consent is often ongoing. Topics can shift. Unexpected sensitive material can come up. Participants can realize midway through that a story feels more personal than they expected.

So ethical consent is not just a document. It is a conversation you revisit. That might mean checking in during an interview, reminding participants they can skip questions, and clarifying that they can stop at any time. It also means using language that participants actually understand. If consent language is full of technical terms, participants might sign without comprehension, and that undermines autonomy.

Now let us talk about confidentiality and anonymity. People often confuse these concepts. Confidentiality means you protect participant information from disclosure. Anonymity means no one, including the researcher, can identify the participant. In many qualitative studies, full anonymity is impossible because you know who you interviewed. So the goal is confidentiality, which requires careful handling of data.

Here is the complication: qualitative data can identify people even if you remove names. Details about a job title, a location, a specific incident, or a timeline can be enough to reveal identity, especially in small communities. That is why de-identification is more than deleting names. It can involve changing or generalizing nonessential details. It can involve using broader categories. It can involve removing unique phrases that reveal identity. The goal is to protect identity while preserving meaning.

Data security matters too. Ethical confidentiality includes secure storage, access control, and careful decisions about who can view raw data. It also includes thinking about where recordings and transcripts live, how they are backed up, and whether they are encrypted.

Now let us address power dynamics. Qualitative research is relational. The researcher often asks personal questions and invites disclosure. Power dynamics can shape what participants say, what they withhold, and whether they feel pressure to participate.

Power can come from many sources: institutional authority, social status, age, professional role, or perceived expertise. For example, if you are interviewing students and you also teach them, they may feel pressure to participate or to give answers they think you want. If you are interviewing employees and their manager supports the study, employees may worry about consequences. Ethical practice requires you to reduce coercion. That might mean using neutral recruiters, avoiding direct recruitment of people you supervise, and clearly stating that participation has no impact on evaluation or relationships.

Now, let us discuss minimizing harm when sensitive topics arise. If you study discrimination, trauma, grief, addiction, or mental health, some distress is possible. Distress does not automatically mean the research is unethical, but it does mean you need a plan.

A plan can include setting expectations before the interview, reminding participants they can pause, skip, or stop, and offering breaks. It can include having a list of support resources available. It can include a protocol for what you will do if someone discloses current risk. The exact response depends on context and professional boundaries, but the key point is that you should not improvise under pressure. You should plan.

This leads to another important ethical concept: boundaries. Researchers are not therapists, unless the study is explicitly designed that way and the researcher is qualified and assigned to that role. Participants may still ask for help, advice, or intervention. A responsible researcher clarifies what they can and cannot do. They can listen, provide resources, and encourage support. They should not promise outcomes they cannot deliver. Boundaries protect participants from false expectations and protect the integrity of the research relationship.

Now let us talk about reflexivity again, because ethics and reflexivity are connected. Reflexivity includes awareness of how your identity, assumptions, and reactions influence what participants share. If you react with surprise, skepticism, or approval, participants may adjust what they say. Ethical interviewing includes self-awareness and a commitment to respectful, nonjudgmental listening.

Representation is another ethical responsibility. In qualitative research, you often use direct quotes and narratives. That creates a responsibility to represent participants fairly and avoid using quotes in ways that distort meaning. It also creates a responsibility to avoid sensationalizing sensitive experiences. Ethical representation asks: are you telling this story in a way that respects the participant's intent and dignity?

Finally, ethics includes transparency. Researchers should document ethical decisions and be able to explain them. If you changed your consent process mid-study, you should document why. If you altered identifying details, you should document what you did and how you preserved meaning. That documentation is part of credibility.

So how do you bring all of this into practice? A useful way is to treat ethics like a research skill. You plan for it, you practice it, you document it, and you revisit it.

If you want a simple checklist to remember, think of four categories: consent, privacy, power, and harm. For each category, ask: what is the risk, and what is my control? That mindset will help you design ethical studies that are both respectful and credible.

Let us close with reflection questions.

First, think about a topic you would like to study qualitatively. What ethical risks are most likely? Consent misunderstandings? Privacy concerns? Power dynamics? Potential distress?

Second, what practical controls would you use? Would you revisit consent during the interview? Would you use stronger de-identification? Would you separate recruitment from authority? Would you create a distress protocol and provide resources?

Third, consider representation. If you were writing findings, how would you protect participant dignity while still communicating the meaning of what they shared?

These reflection questions help you practice ethical planning as an active part of research design, not an afterthought.
