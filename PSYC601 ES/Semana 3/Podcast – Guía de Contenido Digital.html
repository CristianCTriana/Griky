Welcome back to PSYC601: Qualitative Methods. This is Week 3, and the topic is Interviewing as Structured Creative Conversation. Interviewing is one of the most common and powerful tools in qualitative research, and it is also one of the most misunderstood. Many people think interviewing is simply talking to someone and asking questions. In qualitative research, interviewing is a method. It requires design, ethical care, and skillful listening, and it is aimed at producing data about meaning, experience, and context.

In Week 2, you focused on ethics, including consent, confidentiality, power dynamics, and minimizing harm. Those ethical habits carry directly into interviewing, because interviews are often where close contact happens. In Week 3, you build a practical skill set. You will learn how to design interview questions that invite depth, how to choose an interview structure, how to build rapport, and how to probe for meaning without leading participants.

You will also focus on the role of the interviewer. The interviewer influences what participants share through wording, tone, pacing, identity, and reactions. That is why reflexivity matters. If you can notice your assumptions and your impulses, you can interview with more clarity and credibility.

By the end of Week 3, you should be able to design a semi-structured interview guide and conduct an interview that produces rich, ethically collected qualitative data.

Let us start with a core idea: qualitative interviewing is structured, but it is also creative. "Structured" does not mean rigid. It means purposeful. An interview is guided by a research question and a plan for what kinds of experiences and meanings you want to explore. "Creative" does not mean improvised. It means responsive. It means you follow the participant's language and meaning, and you remain open to unexpected insights.

So what makes an interview qualitative? The purpose is one difference. In many surveys or structured interviews, the purpose is to capture consistent responses that can be compared easily. In qualitative interviewing, the purpose is to explore depth, context, and interpretation. You want stories, examples, metaphors, and descriptions. You want to understand how the participant makes sense of what happened, not only what happened.

Now let us talk about interview structures, because the structure you choose affects the kind of data you can gather.

A fully structured interview uses the same questions in the same order for every participant, with limited flexibility. This can be useful when comparability is the main goal and when the topic is narrow. But it can limit depth because the interviewer may not follow unexpected but important topics.

A semi-structured interview is the most common format in qualitative research. It includes a set of core open-ended questions, but it allows flexibility through probing and follow-up. This format balances consistency with depth. You can compare topics across participants, but you can also follow meaning when something important appears.

An unstructured interview is more open. It might begin with one broad prompt, and then the conversation flows based on what the participant brings up. This can produce very rich data, but it can be harder to manage, especially for new interviewers or sensitive topics. It can also make comparison across participants more difficult.

So how do you choose? Think about your research question and your participants. If you need to cover certain topics and you want ethical control over sensitive questions, semi-structured is often a strong choice.

Now let us move into question design, because question design is where many interviews rise or fall.

Strong qualitative questions are open-ended. They invite description, not yes-or-no answers. Instead of asking, "Do you feel anxious," you might ask, "Can you tell me about a time when anxiety felt present, and what that was like for you?" Instead of asking, "How often do you use social media," you might ask, "Can you describe how social media fits into a typical day, and what it means to you?"

Another element is neutrality. A neutral question does not assume a conclusion. For example, asking, "How has social media harmed your confidence?" assumes harm. A more neutral question is, "How does social media affect you, if it affects you at all?" Neutrality protects credibility because it reduces the chance that you will steer participants toward a narrative you expect.

Sequencing matters too. Many qualitative interview guides move from broad to specific and from less sensitive to more sensitive. This helps build rapport and gives participants time to ease into the conversation. For example, you might begin with background and general experience, then move into specific incidents, then move into emotions or meaning, and then move into reflection and coping.

Now let us talk about probing. Probing is how you invite depth without taking over the conversation. A probe is a follow-up question that asks for clarification, examples, and meaning. It might sound like, "Can you tell me more about that?" "What did you mean when you said you felt behind?" "Can you give me an example?" "How did that affect you?" "What happened next?"

Probes are powerful because participants often speak in shorthand. They might say, "It was a lot," or "I felt judged," or "I shut down." Your job is to ask what those phrases mean in their lived experience. That is where qualitative data becomes rich.

Listening is the other half of probing. Skilled interviewers listen for meaning, emotion, and language patterns. They notice metaphors, repeated words, and shifts in tone. They also notice what is not said. Silence can be important. Sometimes people need time to remember or to decide how to speak. If you fill every silence with a new question, you may prevent depth.

Now let us connect interviewing to ethics. Ethical interviewing includes consent reminders, respect for boundaries, and awareness of distress. It includes making sure participants know they can skip questions. It includes checking in when a topic becomes sensitive. It includes protecting privacy in how you record and store data. Ethical interviewing also includes understanding your role. Participants may seek advice or support. You should have a plan for how you will respond while keeping boundaries clear.

Reflexivity matters in interviewing too. Your identity, your tone, and your assumptions shape the interaction. If you show approval when a participant says something, they may lean into that perspective. If you react with surprise, they may shut down. Reflexivity means noticing those reactions and staying grounded. It also means reflecting after the interview about what influenced the conversation.

So what does it look like to do this well? It looks like an interview where the participant feels respected, the conversation has a clear purpose, and the interviewer follows meaning with curiosity and care. It also looks like documentation. A good interviewer takes notes about context, reactions, and decisions. Those notes become part of the data.

If you want a practical takeaway for Week 3, it is this: do not try to ask everything. Ask fewer questions, ask them well, and listen deeply. That is what turns a conversation into qualitative data.

Let us close with reflection questions.

First, think of a topic you would like to interview about. What open-ended question would invite a story rather than an opinion? Try to write one prompt that starts with "Tell me about…" or "Can you walk me through…"

Second, think about probes. What follow-up question would invite meaning? "What did that mean to you?" is often more powerful than "Why did you do that?"

Third, consider rapport and neutrality. How would you ask about a sensitive topic in a way that does not impose judgment? How would you create space for participants to describe positive, negative, or mixed experiences?

Finally, consider ethics. What would you say at the beginning of an interview to clarify boundaries, confidentiality, and the option to skip questions?

If you can answer these questions, you are building the practical interviewing mindset that will support your work throughout the course.
