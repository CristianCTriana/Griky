Semana 4
Análisis de Contenido y Medios

 

Welcome back to PSYC601: Qualitative Methods. This is Week 4, and the topic is Content and Media Analysis. Up to this point, you have focused on methods that involve direct interaction with participants, especially interviewing. This week expands your view of data. In content and media analysis, the "participants" are often texts, images, and cultural artifacts that already exist. Instead of asking people what they think, you study the messages that are already circulating in public and organizational life.

Content and media analysis are powerful because so much of human meaning is produced and shared through communication. News articles shape public understanding. Social media frames identity and norms. Advertisements tell stories about what should matter. Organizational emails reveal priorities and assumptions. When you analyze these materials qualitatively, you can understand how issues are framed, how identities are represented, and how values and power dynamics appear in everyday messages.

In Week 4, you will learn how to design a content or media analysis study. That includes choosing a research question, building a dataset of artifacts, creating a coding approach, and analyzing patterns and themes. You will also focus on two important concepts: manifest content, which is what is explicit and visible, and latent content, which is what is implied, assumed, or communicated indirectly.

By the end of the week, you should be able to design a small content or media analysis project and describe how your sampling and coding choices support credibility.

Let us start with what content and media analysis studies. In simple terms, it studies messages. It studies what is said, what is shown, how it is said, and what it implies. The "data" might be written text, images, audio, video, or combinations, such as a social media post with a caption and a video clip. The method you choose depends on the research question.

For example, you might ask: How do news outlets frame adolescent mental health? How do campus wellness emails describe stress and responsibility? How do advertisements use mental health language to sell products? How do online communities represent identity and coping? These questions do not require interviews. The evidence is already present in public or organizational communication.

Now, a key difference between this method and interviewing is that you do not have the ability to probe the artifact. A document cannot answer follow-up questions. That means you have to be careful about context. Where did the artifact come from? Who created it? For what audience? Under what constraints? A social media post is not the same as a policy document. A marketing campaign is not the same as a personal blog. Context shapes meaning.

This is where sampling decisions become extremely important. Sampling is how you select the artifacts you will analyze. In qualitative content analysis, sampling is usually purposeful. You choose artifacts that are relevant to your research question, and you document your selection rules clearly.

A strong sampling plan includes things like: what sources you will use, what time period you will study, what inclusion criteria you will apply, and what you will exclude. For example, you might study all campus wellness emails sent during a semester, or you might study news articles from three major outlets during one month, or you might study posts under a specific hashtag during a defined week. The key is transparency. Readers should be able to understand what your dataset includes and why.

Sampling also shapes what you can claim. If you analyze one week of posts under one hashtag, you can identify patterns in that dataset. You cannot claim that you have captured all social media discourse. That does not make the study weak. It means your conclusions should match your data scope.

Now let us move into coding, which is the backbone of systematic analysis. Coding means labeling segments of data in a way that helps you identify patterns. A codebook is a document that defines your codes, explains when to use them, and often provides examples.

In content and media analysis, your codes can capture different aspects of the artifact. You might code topics, such as "stress," "therapy," "self-care," or "product promotion." You might code tone, such as "supportive," "humorous," "dismissive," or "urgent." You might code framing, such as "individual responsibility," "systemic problem," "medical issue," or "identity expression."

This is where manifest and latent content analysis becomes useful. Manifest content refers to what is explicitly present. If an email says, "Try breathing exercises," that is manifest content. Latent content refers to underlying meaning or assumptions. For example, an email might imply that stress is an individual problem to manage, rather than a structural issue. That assumption is not always stated directly, but it is communicated through language and framing.

Manifest analysis can be more straightforward, but it can miss what matters most. Latent analysis can be more interpretive, which means it requires discipline and reflexivity. You should not treat your first interpretation as truth. You should look for patterns across artifacts and ground your interpretation in evidence.

Reflexivity plays a big role here. Because you are interpreting artifacts, your assumptions can shape what you see. For example, if you are skeptical about wellness culture, you may interpret any wellness message as marketing or blame. If you are supportive of wellness messaging, you may interpret it as helpful even when it places responsibility on individuals. Reflexivity means noticing your reactions, writing notes, and checking whether your interpretations are supported consistently by the data.

Another credibility issue is consistency. Coding should be applied consistently across artifacts. If you change codes mid-way, that can be fine, but you should document the change and explain why. Iteration is common in qualitative work, but transparency is what makes iteration credible.

Now let us talk about ethics, because even though you are not interviewing participants, ethical considerations still exist. If you are analyzing public content, you still need to consider privacy and harm. Public does not always mean harmless. People may not expect their posts to be studied or quoted. In some cases, it may be appropriate to paraphrase rather than quote directly. In other cases, it may be appropriate to remove identifying details even if content is public.

Ethical practice in content and media analysis includes thinking about how your work could affect the people or communities represented. It also includes respecting platform norms and institutional guidelines.

Let us consider a practical example. Suppose you want to analyze short-form videos about anxiety. You could create a dataset of posts using hashtags and a time window. You would define inclusion criteria, such as language, relevance, and content type. Then you would develop a codebook. You might code manifest topics like symptoms, coping, humor, and advice. You might code latent framing like identity, trend, medicalization, or moral judgment. You might code tone and commercial elements. As you code, you would write reflexive notes about your reactions. After coding, you would look for patterns. You might find that coping tips are common but often simplified. You might find that humor reduces stigma but sometimes trivializes. You might find that commercial messaging is intertwined with mental health language. Those findings would be grounded in the dataset and explained clearly.

The overall goal of Week 4 is to help you see texts and media as meaningful data and to analyze them systematically. It is a way to study meaning in the world as it is communicated, not only as it is remembered in interviews.

Let us close with reflection questions.

First, what kind of media or documents might be useful data for a topic you care about? News articles, social media posts, wellness emails, advertisements, policy documents, or online forums are all possibilities.

Second, what would your sampling plan look like? What sources would you include, and what time window would make sense? How would you define inclusion criteria in a way that is clear and defensible?

Third, what codes would you use? Try writing one manifest code and one latent code. The manifest code might capture an explicit topic. The latent code might capture framing or assumption.

Finally, what reflexive notes might you need? What assumptions do you bring to that topic, and how might those assumptions shape your interpretation?

These questions help you practice the core Week 4 skills: sampling, coding, interpretation, and reflexivity.
