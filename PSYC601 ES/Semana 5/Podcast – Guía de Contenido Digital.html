Week 5
Análisis de Narrativa y Conversación

 

Welcome back to PSYC601: Qualitative Methods. This is Week 5, and the topic is Narrative and Conversation Analysis. In earlier weeks, you focused on qualitative research as a way to study meaning, ethics, interviewing, and the analysis of texts and media. This week you shift your attention in a subtle but important way. You are not only interested in what people say. You are interested in how they say it. You are interested in the structure of stories and the organization of talk.

Narrative analysis and conversation analysis are both approaches that treat language as more than a container for information. They treat language as action. People use stories to organize experience, to make sense of what happened, and to present themselves to others. People use conversation to negotiate understanding, manage relationships, and coordinate social life in real time.

In Week 5, you will learn the basics of narrative analysis, including how to identify story elements, turning points, and identity positioning. You will also learn the basics of conversation analysis, including turn taking, sequence, pauses, overlaps, and repairs. You will practice noticing evidence that is not only content, but structure.

By the end of the week, you should be able to explain the difference between narrative and conversation analysis and apply basic tools from both approaches to a transcript.

As you work through this week, a helpful mindset is to slow down. Read a transcript like you are listening closely. Notice what is emphasized, what is repeated, and what is avoided. That is where meaning often lives.

Let us start with narrative analysis. Narrative analysis focuses on stories. A story is not only a long dramatic account. People tell stories constantly, even in short form. "Yesterday I tried to call my mom, and it turned into a fight" is a story. "I thought I was doing fine, but then I broke down in the parking lot" is a story. Stories organize experience by selecting events and arranging them in a meaningful sequence.

Narrative analysis asks: how is the story structured? What is treated as the beginning, the conflict, the turning point, and the outcome? What events are included, and what events are left out? What moral or lesson is implied? How does the storyteller position themselves? Are they the agent who makes choices, or are they the person events happen to? How do they position other people: as supportive, threatening, absent, or misunderstood?

One practical way to start narrative analysis is to identify core story elements. You can look for the setting, which is where and when the story takes place. You can look for characters, including who is present and who is missing. You can look for the plot sequence: what happened first, what happened next, what changed, and what resulted. You can look for the turning point, which is the moment when something shifts. You can also look for how the storyteller frames meaning, such as what they learned, what they regret, what they value, or what they believe the story proves about themselves.

Now, narrative analysis is particularly useful for studying identity. People often use stories to answer the question, "Who am I?" They may not say it directly, but it appears through positioning. For example, someone who describes job loss as a betrayal may position themselves as wronged and powerless. Someone who describes job loss as a wake-up call may position themselves as adaptive and agentic. The event can be similar, but the narrative framing changes the meaning.

Narrative analysis also pays attention to time. People can narrate events as permanent, temporary, cyclical, or transformative. Someone might say, "This is just how my life always goes," which implies permanence and inevitability. Someone else might say, "That was a turning point," which implies transformation. Those time frames matter for how people imagine their future.

Now let us shift to conversation analysis. Conversation analysis studies talk in interaction. It is not focused on what people think internally. It is focused on what people do through talk. It looks at how people take turns, how they respond to each other, and how meaning is negotiated moment by moment.

Turn taking is a basic feature of conversation. People tend to take turns in structured ways, even when the conversation feels informal. Conversation analysis examines how people claim the floor, how they yield it, and how interruptions or overlaps happen. These details can signal power, alignment, conflict, or urgency.

Sequence is another important element. Conversations often have predictable patterns, such as question and answer, invitation and acceptance, complaint and response, story and evaluation. Conversation analysis looks at how those sequences unfold. For example, a person might offer a complaint, and the other person might respond with empathy, with advice, with minimization, or with a topic change. Each response shapes what becomes possible next.

Repairs are another key concept. Repairs are moments when speakers correct themselves, rephrase, clarify, or fix misunderstandings. You hear repairs all the time: "I mean…" "No, that is not what I meant" "Let me say that differently." Repairs are interesting because they reveal where meaning is fragile. They show what the speaker is trying to accomplish and how they adjust their language to do it.

Pauses, timing, and overlap can also matter. A long pause can signal uncertainty, discomfort, or reflection. Overlap can signal enthusiasm, agreement, or competition for the floor. In conversation analysis, these timing details can be evidence, which is why transcription can be very detailed in this approach.

Now, you might be wondering how these methods apply to interviews, since interviews are not natural everyday conversation. Interviews are structured interactions. That structure shapes what is said and how. In interviews, the interviewer often controls turn taking through questions. But you still see interaction patterns. You still see hesitation, repairs, and negotiation of meaning. If an interviewee says, "I do not know how to explain it," and then tries again, that is identity and meaning work happening in the moment.

So what is the practical difference between narrative analysis and conversation analysis? Narrative analysis focuses on story structure and meaning framing. Conversation analysis focuses on interaction structure and how talk is organized. They can overlap, but they are not the same.

Let us take a simple example. Suppose a participant tells a story about a conflict with a supervisor. Narrative analysis would examine the story arc: how the supervisor is portrayed, what the turning point was, and what the story implies about the participant's identity and values. Conversation analysis would examine how the story is told in interaction: where pauses occur, how the interviewer responds, whether the participant repairs or rephrases, and how agreement or disagreement is negotiated.

Both approaches require care in representation. When you quote a story or a conversational segment, you are sharing someone's words. That creates ethical responsibility to protect privacy and to avoid using excerpts out of context. It also requires transparency about how you analyzed the data. In narrative and conversation analysis, small excerpts can carry a lot of meaning, so you must show how you interpreted them.

If you want a simple takeaway for Week 5, it is this: meaning is not only in topics. Meaning is in structure. It is in turning points, in identity positioning, in repairs, in pauses, and in how people respond to each other. When you learn to see that, you gain a deeper understanding of human experience as it is expressed in language.

Let us close with reflection questions.

First, think about a story someone has told you recently. What was the turning point? How did the person position themselves in the story? Did they present themselves as agentic, helpless, resilient, guilty, or proud?

Second, think about a moment of misunderstanding in a conversation. What repair happened? How did the speaker correct or clarify? What did that reveal about what mattered to them?

Third, if you were analyzing a transcript, what evidence would you use to support a claim? In narrative analysis, you might use excerpts that show turning points or identity language. In conversation analysis, you might use excerpts that show turn taking patterns or repairs.

These reflection questions help you practice Week 5 listening: slower, more attentive, and more sensitive to structure.
