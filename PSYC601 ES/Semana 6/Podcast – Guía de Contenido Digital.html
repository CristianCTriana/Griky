Semana 6
Exploraciones en Marcos Analíticos

 

Welcome back to PSYC601: Qualitative Methods. This is Week 6, and the topic is Explorations in Analytical Frameworks. By this point in the course, you have talked about interviews, ethics, media artifacts, and language-focused approaches like narrative and conversation analysis. Now you turn to a question that every qualitative researcher faces sooner or later: what do you do with all of the data once you have it? Qualitative datasets can feel rich and exciting, but they can also feel overwhelming. Analytical frameworks help you turn that richness into credible interpretation.

Let us start with the problem frameworks are designed to solve. Imagine you have five interview transcripts. Each transcript is twenty pages. You have field notes from observations. You have documents, emails, or posts. You might have recordings you still need to transcribe. If you try to hold all of that in your head, you will either get lost or you will jump too quickly to conclusions based on the most memorable moments.

Analytical frameworks give you structure. They are not the same thing as theory, and they are not rigid templates that force data to fit a predetermined answer. A framework is a set of guiding steps and tools that help you organize, compare, refine, and explain your interpretations. In other words, frameworks help you make your reasoning visible.

In Week 6, you will explore how frameworks support coding, theme development, comparison across cases, memo writing, and documentation of analytic choices. By the end of the week, you should be able to select an approach that fits your research question and apply it in a way that strengthens credibility.

A useful starting point is to think of analysis as a chain. You start with data, which might be a transcript excerpt, an observation note, or a paragraph from a document. You then create codes, which are labels that capture meaning, action, or concept. You then organize codes into themes, which are broader patterns that connect multiple pieces of data. You then write claims, which are interpretations that answer the research question. Finally, you support claims with evidence, which includes direct excerpts that demonstrate how the theme appears in the data.

This chain sounds simple, but it solves a major credibility problem. It prevents analysis from becoming a list of opinions. It also prevents analysis from becoming a collection of quotes with no interpretation. Instead, it creates a pathway that a reader can follow.

Now let us talk about coding, because coding is often the first concrete step in many frameworks. Coding is the process of marking segments of data and labeling them in a way that helps you recognize patterns. Early in analysis, coding is usually broad. You might label a segment "feeling judged," "seeking support," "conflict with expectations," or "trying to cope." Some frameworks encourage you to use participants' own language for early codes, because it keeps you close to the data and reduces premature interpretation.

As coding continues, the task shifts. You begin to notice which codes appear across multiple cases and which codes are rare or context-specific. You begin to compare how the same concept appears in different participants. For example, "support" might mean emotional encouragement for one participant and financial help for another. Constant comparison, which is a common feature across many analytic approaches, helps you see those differences and refine your understanding.

Refinement is another important step. Many people think that once a code exists, it should never change. In qualitative work, codes often evolve. You might merge two overlapping codes. You might split a broad code into more precise sub-codes. You might rename a code as you understand it better. The key is not to avoid change. The key is to document change.

This is where memo writing becomes central. Analytic memos are short written reflections that capture your reasoning as it develops. A memo might answer questions like: Why am I merging these codes. What is the difference between these two themes. What assumptions am I bringing into this interpretation. What evidence supports this claim. Memos are not busywork. They are the place where analysis becomes explicit. They also create a record that helps you explain your process to others.

Another useful concept is the audit trail. An audit trail is a record of what you did, when you did it, and why you made changes. It can include versions of your codebook, notes about coding revisions, memos, and decisions about sampling or data selection. The audit trail is a credibility tool because it shows that your findings did not appear magically. They emerged through a transparent process.

Now let us talk about framework fit, because not every analysis goal is the same. Some studies are descriptive. They aim to summarize patterns and categories. Other studies are interpretive. They aim to explain meaning, identity, and context. Other studies focus on process. They aim to describe how something unfolds over time, including stages, turning points, and shifts.

If your question asks, "What are common experiences of first-generation students seeking support," a descriptive and interpretive approach might work well. If your question asks, "How do people adapt after job loss over time," a process-oriented approach might be more fitting. If your question asks, "How is identity constructed through storytelling," a narrative framework may be most appropriate. Frameworks are tools, so the tool should match the task.

It can also help to distinguish between frameworks and theories. A theory is often a set of concepts and relationships that explain a phenomenon. A framework is the structured approach you use to analyze data. They can connect. For example, you might use a theory to inform what you look for, but you still need a framework to guide how you code, compare, and build claims. You can also do inductive analysis, where themes emerge from the data without starting from a strong theoretical lens. Even then, you still use structured steps. Inductive does not mean unstructured.

A common mistake is to treat analysis like a scavenger hunt for quotes that support a conclusion you already believe. Frameworks help prevent that by encouraging iterative comparison and by requiring evidence for claims. Another common mistake is to treat analysis like a summary of everything participants said. Frameworks help prevent that by pushing you to identify patterns that answer the research question, not simply repeat the data.

Let us make this practical. Imagine you are studying help-seeking among college students. You have interviews where students talk about anxiety, stigma, friends, counseling services, and time constraints. Without a framework, you might simply list those topics. With a framework, you might begin with initial codes like "fear of judgment," "uncertainty about services," "self-reliance," and "relief after disclosure." You might compare across cases and discover that "self-reliance" functions differently depending on family expectations. You might refine themes into something like "earning the right to seek help" or "managing identity risk." You might then support those themes with excerpts and show how your coding decisions led to your claims.

Notice what changed. You did not lose the richness of the data. You gained a structure that makes your interpretation clear and defensible.

Week 6 is also a reminder that qualitative analysis is not only about technique. It is also about judgment. Frameworks do not eliminate the researcher's role. They make the researcher's role more transparent. They help you be honest about the choices you made and the reasoning you used. That honesty is part of rigor.

If you want one phrase to carry into the rest of the course, it is this: make your logic visible. When you do that, your analysis becomes easier to communicate, easier to evaluate, and easier to trust.

Let us close with reflection questions.

First, think about a small piece of qualitative data you have seen, maybe a short interview excerpt or a paragraph from a document. What is one initial code you would apply, using the participant's language when possible.

Second, what might that code connect to in a broader theme. What would the theme be called, and how would you define it.

Third, what claim would that theme support, and what excerpt would you use as evidence. If you cannot find evidence, that is a sign your claim needs revision.

Finally, consider documentation. If you changed a code or merged themes, how would you record the reason. What memo would you write so your future self, or a reader, could follow your reasoning.

These questions help you practice Week 6 skills: coding, comparison, memo writing, and transparent interpretation.
