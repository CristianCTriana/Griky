Semana 7
Comprensión de la Etnografía y la Investigación de Campo

 

Welcome back to PSYC601: Qualitative Methods. This is Week 7, and the topic is Understanding Ethnography and Field Research. Up to this point in the course, you have worked with interviews, texts, media, and analytic frameworks. This week shifts your attention to a method that is deeply connected to context. Ethnography and field research are about studying social life as it unfolds, in the settings where it actually happens.

Ethnography is often described as the study of culture, but culture here does not only mean nationality or ethnicity. Culture can also mean the norms and routines of a group, the unspoken rules of a workplace, the interaction patterns of a community, or the shared meaning system of an organization. Ethnography helps you understand how those norms and meanings shape behavior.

Field research is a broader term that includes ethnographic approaches. The core idea is that you spend time in a setting and learn through observation, participation, and relationship building. Instead of relying only on what people say about their lives, you see how life is organized in practice.

In Week 7, you focus on field entry, the role of the researcher, positionality, and fieldnotes as data. You also connect ethnography to ethics, because being present in people's spaces creates ongoing responsibilities. By the end of the week, you should be able to design a basic field research plan and explain what makes fieldwork credible.

Let us start with what ethnography is designed to capture. Ethnography is interested in the taken-for-granted. It is interested in what people do without explaining it because it feels normal to them. Many norms are invisible when you only interview people, because people forget to mention what feels obvious. Fieldwork helps you see those patterns.

For example, imagine you are studying belonging among first-year college students. If you ask students directly, they might describe friends, clubs, or loneliness. Those are valuable. But in the field, you might notice subtle patterns: who saves seats for whom, who gets greeted by name, who lingers after meetings, who gets included in decision making, and who remains on the edge of a group. Those patterns can reveal how belonging is built or blocked in daily interaction.

Now, fieldwork requires entry, and entry is not only logistical. It is relational and ethical. You need to think about access and gatekeepers. Gatekeepers are individuals or structures that control entry to a setting. In a school, it might be administrators. In a clinic, it might be supervisors and ethics boards. In a community organization, it might be leaders. Ethical entry means you clarify your purpose, obtain appropriate permissions, and communicate in a way that respects the setting.

Entry also includes role decisions. Will you be an observer only, or will you participate in some activities. How visible will you be. What will you tell people about your role. These decisions influence what you see and how people respond to you.

This leads to a major concept: the researcher is never invisible. Your presence influences the setting. People may act differently when they know they are being observed. This is not a reason to abandon ethnography. It is a reason to practice reflexivity. You document how your presence may shape what happens, and you interpret your findings with that awareness.

Repeated engagement can also reduce observer effects. When people see you regularly, your presence becomes part of the environment. This is one reason ethnography often involves time. You need time not only to collect more data, but also to understand context and build trust.

Now let us talk about positionality. Positionality refers to how your identity and social location shape your access, your relationships, and your interpretations. You might be an insider in some ways and an outsider in others. For example, if you are studying a youth program and you are an adult, you may be an outsider in age and authority. If you share a cultural background, you may be closer in other ways. Positionality affects what people share with you and what they assume you understand.

Reflexivity is how you manage positionality. Reflexivity means writing about your assumptions, reactions, and influence. It means noticing where you feel comfortable and where you feel awkward. It means noticing what you pay attention to and what you overlook. Reflexivity is not self-indulgence. It is a credibility practice. It shows that you are aware of your role as an instrument of research.

Fieldnotes are the primary data in ethnography, so fieldnote quality matters. Many new field researchers write notes that are too general, like "the atmosphere was welcoming." That might be true, but it is not evidence. Evidence comes from thick description. Thick description includes details about setting, sequence, interaction, language, and context.

A strong fieldnote often separates three kinds of writing. First is descriptive writing: what you saw, who was there, what happened, what was said. Second is analytic writing: what you think it might mean, what patterns you notice, what questions emerge. Third is reflexive writing: how you felt, what assumptions you brought, how your presence might have influenced events.

This structure helps you avoid mixing interpretation into description too early. It also helps you analyze later, because you can return to descriptive notes as evidence and use analytic notes as early hypotheses.

Now let us talk about ethics in the field, because fieldwork creates ongoing ethical decisions. You might not have a single consent moment like in an interview. Consent can be more complex. In some settings, you may obtain formal consent from participants or organizations. In other settings, especially public spaces, observation might be ethically acceptable without formal consent, but you still have responsibilities. You must think about privacy, harm, and identification risk.

You should be careful about recording identifying details. You should consider whether people reasonably expect privacy in that setting. You should think about whether documenting an interaction could create risk if it were recognized. You should also be prepared for sensitive moments. If a conflict escalates, if someone discloses harm, or if people become uncomfortable, you may need to pause, shift your focus, or leave. Ethical fieldwork is not passive. It includes active decisions to minimize harm.

Another ethical issue is boundaries. When you spend time in a setting, relationships can form. People may start to see you as a friend, helper, or ally. That can be meaningful, but it can also blur roles. A field researcher should be clear about their role and should avoid making promises they cannot keep. Boundaries protect both participants and the integrity of the research.

Now, how does analysis work in ethnography. Ethnographic analysis often involves identifying patterns in routines, norms, and meaning systems. You might code fieldnotes, compare across sites or time periods, and build themes. Your analytic memos become important here, because they capture emerging interpretations and help you refine your understanding.

A key strength of ethnography is that it can reveal contradictions between what people say and what they do. That does not mean people are dishonest. It means social life is complex. People might value inclusion in principle while still practicing subtle exclusion. Fieldwork can reveal those tensions and help you understand how norms operate in practice.

If you want a simple summary of Week 7, it is this: ethnography teaches you to study the social world as it is lived, not only as it is described. It requires time, thick description, reflexivity, and ethical care. When done well, it produces a kind of understanding that is difficult to achieve through interviews alone.

Let us close with reflection questions.

First, think of a setting you could observe ethically. What norms do you suspect operate there? What routines shape behavior?

Second, what would you write in fieldnotes? What would count as description? What would count as interpretation? What would count as reflexive reflection?

Third, consider positionality. How might your identity affect access and interaction in that setting? What would people assume about you and how might that shape what they do?

Finally, consider ethics. What privacy risks exist, and how would you minimize them? What boundaries would you set so your role stays clear?

These questions help you practice ethnographic thinking: slow observation, careful documentation, and awareness of context and responsibility.
