Choosing Your Statistics

Choosing the proper statistical test(s) for your quantitative dataset is critical for making reliable and valid conclusions about your hypothesis. Choose tests that align with your ability to interpret the results and explain them in the context of your research. In addition, ensure that the statistical tests you choose are supported by your software tools (e.g., SPSS, R, SAS, Excel).

As you look at the array of statistical tests available, ask yourself what you are trying to discover. Are you testing a relationship between (or among) variables, comparing the responses between two groups, or trying to make predictions? Knowing what you are trying to discover will help guide you to the most appropriate statistical test(s).

Reflect on the hypothesis for your study. What statistical test(s) seem well designed to support or refute your hypothesis? In addition, you'll need to consider the following aspects of your dataset and how you intend to analyze the data.

How have the variables of interest been measured in your online dataset? Do the data use nominal (e.g., gender), ordinal (Likert-type rating scales), interval (e.g., temperature), ratio (e.g., height, weight) forms of measurement, or some combination of these? Different statistical tests will be used for different types of measurement.

How is your data distributed? Is it distributed in a normal (bell curve) fashion? Many parametric tests assume normality. Consider using visual methods (e.g., histograms, Q-Q plots) or statistical tests (e.g., Shapiro-Wilk test) to determine the evaluation of the distribution of your data. When making statistical comparisons across groups (e.g., ANOVA), check if variances are equal across groups since some statistical tests assume that.

Nonparametric tests or adjustments might be necessary for small samples because some statistical tests are sensitive to sample size and may not perform well with small datasets. Conversely, large sample sizes often allow parametric tests and can provide more robust results.

When comparing two groups, use inferential statistical tests like a t-test for comparing means or a chi-square test for comparing categorical data. Consider using ANOVA to compare means among more than two groups when comparing multiple groups. The Kruskal-Wallis test is appropriate for comparing multiple groups with ordinal data. Consider statistical tests like multiple regression, MANOVA, or factor analysis when analyzing relationships among several variables.

When summarizing data, use descriptive statistics such as mean, median, mode, standard deviation, and variance. Correlation coefficients (e.g., Pearson's r) and regression analysis (e.g., linear regression) were used to explore relationships between variables.

For data collected from the same subjects over time, use tests like paired t-tests or repeated measures ANOVA. For comparing different groups, use independent samples like t-tests or one-way like ANOVA.

Each statistical test has assumptions (e.g., normality, independence, and equal variances). Ensure your data meets these assumptions, or consider alternative tests if assumptions are violated. If assumptions for parametric tests are not met, non-parametric tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney U test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) can be used as they do not rely on distributional assumptions.

Consider the effect size you want to detect. Some tests allow for calculating the effect size (e.g., Cohen's d, eta squared). Conduct a power analysis to determine the sample size needed to detect a meaningful effect.
